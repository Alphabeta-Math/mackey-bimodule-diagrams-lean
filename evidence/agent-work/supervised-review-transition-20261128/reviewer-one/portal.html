Bimodule String Diagrams and the Generalized Mackey Decomposition

By Ian Xiao · November 26, 2026 · 25 min read

I develop a string-diagram calculus for group-algebra bimodules in which centralizer cosets record the conjugation embeddings used in induction and restriction. Explicit inclusion and projection maps give Mackey decomposition for conjugate embeddings. I prove Reidemeister I and II identities and construct fixed-boundary Reidemeister III replacements. Some cases require sums, and others admit no replacement even by linear combinations. For finite parabolic subgroups of \(S_\infty\), ordered block matching gives explicit double-coset representatives and Mackey summands. Parabolic intersections and joins give further identities. For the move with a middle restriction strand, a stabilizer index determines the attainable orbit coefficients on the right-hand shape over any commutative coefficient ring.

1 Bimodules and functors

We define the strands and generating maps as group-algebra bimodules and use Eilenberg–Watts to identify the corresponding functors and natural transformations.

1.1 The Eilenberg–Watts correspondence

For unital rings \(R,S\), \(R\text{-}\mathrm{Mod}\) denotes the category of unital left \(R\)-modules and \(\operatorname{Bimod}(S,R)\) the category of unital \(S\)-\(R\) bimodules.

Theorem 1.1 (Eilenberg–Watts [1, 2]). For unital rings \(R,S\), there is an equivalence from \(\operatorname{Bimod}(S,R)\) to the category of additive, right-exact functors \(R\text{-}\mathrm{Mod}\to S\text{-}\mathrm{Mod}\) that preserve all set-indexed coproducts, with natural transformations as morphisms. It sends \[B\longmapsto B\otimes_R-,\qquad f\longmapsto f\otimes_R\mathrm{id}.\] Its quasi-inverse sends \(F\) to \(F({}_R R)\) and \(\theta\) to \(\theta_R\). For \(b\in F(R)\) and \(r\in R\), the right action is \(br=F(\rho_r)(b)\), where \(\rho_r(x)=xr\). For composable bimodules \(B\in\operatorname{Bimod}(S,R)\) and \(C\in\operatorname{Bimod}(T,S)\), composition corresponds to \(C\otimes_S B\), and the identity corresponds to the regular bimodule.

For \(\mathbb C\)-algebras the equivalence identifies bimodules with equal scalar actions with \(\mathbb C\)-linear functors of the stated kind.

Let \(\mathcal A,\mathcal E\) be locally small abelian categories with all set-indexed coproducts and small projective generators \(P,Q\). Here small means that \(\operatorname{Hom}(P,-)\) or \(\operatorname{Hom}(Q,-)\) preserves those coproducts. Put \(R=\operatorname{End}_{\mathcal A}(P)^\mathrm{op}\) and \(S=\operatorname{End}_{\mathcal E}(Q)^\mathrm{op}\). The Hom functors give equivalences \(\mathcal A\simeq R\text{-}\mathrm{Mod}\) and \(\mathcal E\simeq S\text{-}\mathrm{Mod}\) [2, Chs. 8–10]. Theorem 1.1 then sends \(F\) to \(\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal E}(Q,F(P))\), with actions \[q^\mathrm{op}b p^\mathrm{op}=F(p)\circ b\circ q.\] A natural transformation acts by postcomposition with its component at \(P\).

1.2 The diagrammatic bimodule category

Khovanov’s graphical calculus for induction and restriction between finite groups is the starting point for this construction [5, § 3.2].

Fix a group \(U\). For \(X\le U\), write \(\mathbb C[X]\) for its complex group algebra. If \({}^{\alpha}\!H=\alpha H\alpha^{-1}\le G\), write \(\alpha_*:\mathbb C[H]\to \mathbb C[G]\) for conjugation by \(\alpha\), extended linearly. This map depends exactly on \[\bar\alpha=\alpha C_U(H),\qquad C_U(H)=\{u\in U:uh=hu\text{ for all }h\in H\}.\] The centralizer in a strand label is taken for the source of its embedding. Thus an eligible conjugating element for a strand from \(H\) into \(G\) satisfies \(\alpha H\alpha^{-1}\le G\); the strand records its centralizer coset, and hence the embedding, rather than a chosen representative.

The up and down strands below denote \[\mathsf I_{\overline{\alpha}}^{G,H}={}_{\mathbb C[G]}(\mathbb C[G])_{\alpha_*,\mathbb C[H]},\qquad \mathsf D_{\overline{\alpha}}^{H,G}={}_{\mathbb C[H],\alpha_*}(\mathbb C[G])_{\mathbb C[G]}.\] The actions are \(x\cdot h=x\alpha h\alpha^{-1}\) on \(\mathsf I\) and \(h\cdot x=\alpha h\alpha^{-1}x\) on \(\mathsf D\). Tensoring on the right gives induction and restriction, respectively. The target group is on the left. Read horizontal composition as tensor product over the middle group and vertical composition from bottom to top.

Nodes.

Let \(\overline{\alpha},\overline{\beta}\) be two labels for embeddings of \(H\) into \(G\). Choose \(\alpha'\in\overline{\alpha}\) and \(\beta'\in\overline{\beta}\) with \(d=\alpha'(\beta')^{-1}\in G\). This determines a node \[\mathsf I_{\overline{\alpha}}^{G,H}\xrightarrow{\ \cdot d\ }\mathsf I_{\overline{\beta}}^{G,H}, \qquad x\longmapsto xd.\] The map respects the right action exactly when \(\alpha_*(h)d=d\beta_*(h)\) for all \(h\in H\). The possible group elements are \(G\cap\alpha C_U(H)\beta^{-1}\). Changing representatives can change \(d\) and hence the map. For the down strands the same \(d\) gives \(x\mapsto d^{-1}x\).

Linear dot labels.

We also allow a node to be a finite \(\mathbb C\)-linear combination of the group-element nodes just defined. For fixed incoming and outgoing labels, write \[a=\sum_{d\in S}c_d d,\qquad c_d\in\mathbb C,\qquad S\subseteq G\cap\alpha C_U(H)\beta^{-1}\text{ finite}.\] Every supported group element must be eligible individually: \(\alpha_*(h)d=d\beta_*(h)\) for every \(h\in H\). On up strands this node acts by \(x\mapsto xa\). The corresponding linear combination on down strands acts by \[x\longmapsto\sum_{d\in S}c_d d^{-1}x.\] This extends the group-element rule linearly; it does not take a multiplicative inverse of \(a\). A group-algebra element that intertwines only after its terms are added need not be an eligible single dot. Larger diagrams can realize further bimodule maps, as in Proposition 3.3.

A linear label occupies one marked node, including for a zero label. Its coefficients and support do not change the drawing’s shape. An eligible node labelled \(1\) evaluates to the identity map of its strand, while the drawing still has a dot. Expanding a label into terms is an equality of evaluated linear combinations; it does not replace one marked node by several successive nodes.

Cups and caps.

Suppress the embedding and group labels on \(\mathsf I,\mathsf D\). Each tensor product is over the group algebra of the middle region. For \(\operatorname{Ind}_{\alpha_*}\dashv\operatorname{Res}_{\alpha_*}\), the unit and counit are \[\begin{aligned} \eta &: \mathbb C[H]\longrightarrow \mathsf D\otimes_{\mathbb C[G]}\mathsf I,\qquad h\longmapsto \alpha_*(h)\otimes 1,\\ \varepsilon&: \mathsf I\otimes_{\mathbb C[H]}\mathsf D\longrightarrow \mathbb C[G],\qquad x\otimes y\longmapsto xy. \end{aligned}\] In the unit formula, \(\mathsf D\otimes_{\mathbb C[G]}\mathsf I\) identifies with \(\mathbb C[G]\) by multiplication. Define the \(\mathbb C[H]\)-bimodule trace \[E_\alpha(g)= \begin{cases} \alpha^{-1}g\alpha,&g\in{}^{\alpha}\!H,\\ 0,&g\notin{}^{\alpha}\!H, \end{cases} \qquad g\in G,\] and extend it linearly. When \([G:{}^{\alpha}\!H]<\infty\), choose \(T\subseteq G\) with \(G=\bigsqcup_{t\in T}{}^{\alpha}\!H\,t\). The reverse adjunction \(\operatorname{Res}_{\alpha_*}\dashv\operatorname{Ind}_{\alpha_*}\) has unit and counit \[\begin{aligned} \widetilde{\eta}&: \mathbb C[G]\longrightarrow \mathsf I\otimes_{\mathbb C[H]}\mathsf D,\qquad g\longmapsto \sum_{t\in T}g t^{-1}\otimes t =\sum_{t\in T}t^{-1}\otimes t g,\\ \widetilde{\varepsilon}&: \mathsf D\otimes_{\mathbb C[G]}\mathsf I\longrightarrow \mathbb C[H],\qquad x\otimes y\longmapsto E_\alpha(xy). \end{aligned}\] Replacing \(t\) by \(\alpha_*(h)t\) leaves the balanced tensor \(t^{-1}\otimes t\) unchanged. Right multiplication by \(g\in G\) permutes the cosets \({}^{\alpha}\!Ht\), which proves the displayed equality for \(\widetilde{\eta}\). The dual-basis identities are \[\sum_{t\in T}t^{-1}\alpha_*\bigl(E_\alpha(t x)\bigr)=x, \qquad \sum_{t\in T}\alpha_*\bigl(E_\alpha(x t^{-1})\bigr)t=x .\] For a basis element \(x\in G\), each identity has exactly one nonzero term, by the decompositions using \(T\) and \(T^{-1}\). They give the two reverse triangle identities. The trace cap is defined at every index. The reverse unit displayed above is part of the generating calculus at finite index.

Merges and splits.

For \({}^{\beta}\!L\le H\) and \({}^{\alpha}\!H\le G\), the induction merge is \[\mathsf I_{\overline{\alpha}}^{G,H}\otimes_{\mathbb C[H]}\mathsf I_{\overline{\beta}}^{H,L} \ \xrightarrow{\;\sim\;}\ \mathsf I_{\overline{\alpha\beta}}^{G,L}, \qquad x\otimes y\longmapsto x\,\alpha_*(y).\] The restriction merge has the reverse order of the two factors, \[\mathsf D_{\overline{\beta}}^{L,H}\otimes_{\mathbb C[H]}\mathsf D_{\overline{\alpha}}^{H,G} \ \xrightarrow{\;\sim\;}\ \mathsf D_{\overline{\alpha\beta}}^{L,G}, \qquad y\otimes x\longmapsto\alpha_*(y)\,x.\] The splits are their inverses, \(z\mapsto z\otimes1\) and \(z\mapsto1\otimes z\), respectively. Reflect the merge diagrams vertically while keeping each strand type to draw the splits.

Diagrams and evaluated maps.

For fixed endpoints \(K,H\), the objects of \(\mathcal D_U(K,H)\) are sequences of strands from \(H\) to \(K\), interpreted as tensor products. When \(K=H\), the empty sequence gives the regular bimodule. Diagrams are built from dots, cups, caps, merges, splits, identities, and the associativity and unit maps for tensor products, using horizontal tensor product and vertical stacking. Each diagram has a specified bottom source and top target. Stacking requires matching boundary objects, including the ordered strands, orientations, centralizer cosets, and region groups.

Fix boundary objects \(X,Y\), also writing \(X,Y\) for their bimodules. Let \(V(X,Y)\) be the vector space of finite formal \(\mathbb C\)-linear combinations of admissible diagrams from \(X\) to \(Y\). For stacking we make the usual associativity and identity-wire identifications. Evaluation is the linear map \[\operatorname{ev}_{X,Y}:V(X,Y)\longrightarrow\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb C[K]\text{-}\mathbb C[H]}(X,Y),\qquad \sum_i c_i\mathsf D_i\longmapsto\sum_i c_i\operatorname{ev}(\mathsf D_i).\] A formal diagram and the bimodule map it evaluates to are distinct objects. Different diagrams can give the same map. We identify two linear combinations exactly when their evaluations agree, so \[\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal D_U(K,H)}(X,Y)=V(X,Y)/\ker\operatorname{ev}_{X,Y}\cong\operatorname{im}\operatorname{ev}_{X,Y}.\] Thus each hom-space is a complex vector space of generated bimodule maps. Representability of further bimodule maps requires a separate argument, such as the orbit-sum construction in Proposition 3.3.

Composition and the diagram algebra.

Stacking extends bilinearly: \[\left(\sum_j b_j\mathsf E_j\right)\circ\left(\sum_i c_i\mathsf D_i\right)=\sum_{j,i}b_jc_i(\mathsf E_j\circ\mathsf D_i),\qquad \mathsf D_i:X\to Y,\quad\mathsf E_j:Y\to Z.\] Evaluation preserves composition. Consequently it descends to the quotient hom-spaces, with associative composition and identity given by the undecorated identity diagram. Horizontal tensor product is also bilinear on maps, over the group algebra of the common region.

To combine diagrams with different sources and targets in one algebra, choose a finite family \(\mathcal X=(X_i)_{i\in F}\) of boundary objects in \(\mathcal D_U(K,H)\), and put \[A_{\mathcal X}=\bigoplus_{i,j\in F}V(X_i,X_j).\] An element of \(V(X_i,X_j)\) occupies block \((j,i)\). Multiply two blocks by stacking when their intermediate boundary indices match, and by zero otherwise; then extend bilinearly. Explicitly, for \(\mathsf D:X_i\to X_j\) and \(\mathsf E:X_k\to X_\ell\), \[\mathsf E_{\ell k}\mathsf D_{ji}=\begin{cases}(\mathsf E\circ\mathsf D)_{\ell i},&k=j,\\0,&k\ne j.\end{cases}\] This is associative matrix multiplication, with identity the sum of the individual identity diagrams in the diagonal blocks.

Evaluate each block as the corresponding bimodule map on \(\bigoplus_{i\in F}X_i\). This gives an algebra homomorphism \(\operatorname{Ev}_{\mathcal X}\) whose kernel is the two-sided ideal \[I_{\mathcal X}=\bigoplus_{i,j\in F}\ker\operatorname{ev}_{X_i,X_j}.\] Indeed, composing an evaluated block map with the inclusion of \(X_i\) and the projection to \(X_j\) recovers its \((j,i)\) entry. The quotient algebra is therefore \[\mathcal A_{\mathcal X}=A_{\mathcal X}/I_{\mathcal X}\cong\bigoplus_{i,j\in F}\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal D_U(K,H)}(X_i,X_j)\cong\operatorname{im}\operatorname{Ev}_{\mathcal X},\] with multiplication as above. It is precisely the algebra of evaluated block maps with diagrammatic entries, and is a unital subalgebra of the bimodule endomorphisms of \(\bigoplus_i X_i\). Every hom-space embeds as its own block. If \(e_i\) is the class of the identity diagram in block \((i,i)\), then \[1=\sum_{i\in F}e_i,\qquad e_i\mathcal A_{\mathcal X}e_i\cong\operatorname{End}_{\mathcal D_U(K,H)}(X_i).\] Thus the fixed-boundary endomorphism algebras are the diagonal corners of the combined diagram algebra.

For an infinite family, the same construction with finite-support block sums is locally unital: any finite collection of elements has a common two-sided unit \(\sum_{i\in S}e_i\), where the finite set \(S\) contains all its source and target indices. With infinitely many nonzero boundary objects there is no global unit in this finite-support algebra. One may adjoin a global unit by adjoining the identity operator on \(\bigoplus_iX_i\) to its evaluated image.

In particular, for one fixed boundary object \(X\), stacking makes \(A_X=V(X,X)\) an associative unital \(\mathbb C\)-algebra, with \(\mathsf E\mathsf D=\mathsf E\circ\mathsf D\) read bottom to top. Evaluation is an algebra homomorphism to the bimodule endomorphisms of \(X\). Its kernel \(I_X\) is a two-sided ideal: for \(n\in I_X\) and \(a,b\in A_X\), \[\operatorname{ev}(anb)=\operatorname{ev}(a)\circ0\circ\operatorname{ev}(b)=0.\] The algebra of diagrammatic endomorphisms is therefore \[\operatorname{End}_{\mathcal D_U(K,H)}(X)=A_X/I_X,\qquad [\mathsf E][\mathsf D]=[\mathsf E\circ\mathsf D],\qquad 1=[\operatorname{id}_X].\] This algebra need not be commutative. On \(\mathsf I_{\bar1}^{G,1}\), every \(g\in G\) gives a dot \(r_g(x)=xg\), and \(r_h\circ r_g(x)=xgh\). If \(gh\ne hg\), the two dot orders give different maps.

Sums of maps and direct sums.

Inside one hom-space, a finite linear combination may be pictured as a superposition of diagrams with the same source and target; evaluation adds their maps on the same input. Actual direct sums of bimodules give different input and output summands: a direct sum of two maps acts separately on the two inputs. One may include finite direct sums of bimodules and matrices of diagrammatic maps. A map \(\bigoplus_i X_i\to\bigoplus_jY_j\) then has entries \(f_{ji}:X_i\to Y_j\), and acts by \((x_i)_i\mapsto(\sum_i f_{ji}(x_i))_j\). This direct sum changes the boundary object, whereas addition takes place in a fixed hom-space. Section 2 uses the full bimodule category for arbitrary direct sums. Its pointwise finite infinite sums have the stated finiteness conditions, beyond the finite combinations defining \(V(X,Y)\).

Convention for diagrammatic identities.

Throughout this article, displayed identities between diagrams are equalities of their evaluated bimodule maps, with the same source and target. For readability we use the slight abuse \(\mathsf D_1=\mathsf D_2\) for \(\operatorname{ev}(\mathsf D_1)=\operatorname{ev}(\mathsf D_2)\). The same convention applies to finite \(\mathbb C\)-linear combinations, with evaluation extended linearly. When an indexed sum is allowed by the stated pointwise finiteness conditions, it denotes the pointwise sum of the evaluated maps: only finitely many terms are nonzero on each input. Such a sum need not belong to \(V(X,Y)\), whose formal linear combinations are finite.

1.3 The corresponding functor category

Eilenberg–Watts identifies \(\mathbb C[K]\)-\(\mathbb C[H]\) bimodules with equal scalar actions with \(\mathbb C\)-linear, right-exact, coproduct-preserving functors from \(\mathbb C[H]\text{-}\mathrm{Mod}\) to \(\mathbb C[K]\text{-}\mathrm{Mod}\). Bimodule maps correspond to natural transformations.

The category \(\mathcal D_U(K,H)\) corresponds to composites of the given inductions and restrictions. Its transformations are built from the indicated maps by tensor product, composition, and finite linear combination. For a transformation between two such tensor functors, membership is determined by its component at \(\mathbb C[H]\), using the tensor unit identifications. Finite direct sums and matrices of maps correspond on both sides.

These composite functors are exact and preserve coproducts. Restriction acts on the same vector spaces, and \(\mathbb C[G]\) is free as a right \(\mathbb C[H]\)-module along each embedding. Allowing every bimodule map would give the full subcategory on these functors.

2 Mackey decomposition

We state ordinary Mackey decomposition and give its diagrammatic form for conjugation embeddings. Each double coset determines one summand and its inclusion and projection.

2.1 The ordinary Mackey theorem

Theorem 2.1 (Ordinary Mackey decomposition [3]). Let \(K,H\le G\) be subgroups with their given inclusions. Set \(I=K\backslash G/H\), the set of double cosets \(KgH=\{kgh:k\in K,\ h\in H\}\) for \(g\in G\). Choose an indexed family \((\sigma_i)_{i\in I}\) in \(G\) with \(\sigma_i\in i\) for each \(i\). Thus \(i=K\sigma_i H\) and \[G=\bigsqcup_{i\in I}K\sigma_i H .\] For each \(i\in I\), set \(L_i=K\cap\sigma_i H\sigma_i^{-1}\). Use the inclusion \(L_i\hookrightarrow K\) and the embedding \(\mu_i:L_i\hookrightarrow H\) given by \(\ell\mapsto\sigma_i^{-1}\ell\sigma_i\). Then there is a natural isomorphism \[\operatorname{Res}_K^G\,\operatorname{Ind}_H^G \ \cong\ \bigoplus_{i\in I}\operatorname{Ind}_{L_i}^K\,\operatorname{Res}_{\mu_i}.\] At the bimodule level the map from the \(i\)-th summand \(\mathbb C[K]\otimes_{\mathbb C[L_i]}\mathbb C[H]\) to \(\mathbb C[G]\) takes \(k\otimes h\) to \(k\sigma_i h\).

The finite-group formulation is from Khovanov [5, Eq. (29) and Prop. 6]. The version for arbitrary groups stated here is the case \(\alpha=\beta=1\) of Theorem 2.2.

2.2 Generalized Mackey theorem for conjugate embeddings

Let \(K,H,G\le U\) and \(\alpha,\beta\in U\) satisfy \({}^{\alpha}\!K\le G\) and \({}^{\beta}\!H\le G\). Let \(J=({}^{\alpha}\!K)\backslash G/({}^{\beta}\!H)\). Choose one representative \(\sigma_j\in j\) for each \(j\in J\), so \[\begin{equation} j=({}^{\alpha}\!K)\sigma_j({}^{\beta}\!H), \qquad G=\bigsqcup_{j\in J}j. \end{equation}\tag{2.1}\] For each \(j\), set \[\begin{equation} L_j={}^{\alpha}\!K\cap{}^{\sigma_j\beta}\!H . \end{equation}\tag{2.2}\] The embeddings out of \(L_j\) are \(\lambda_j(\ell)=\alpha^{-1}\ell\alpha\in K\) and \(\mu_j(\ell)=\beta^{-1}\sigma_j^{-1}\ell\sigma_j\beta\in H\). The strand labels for these maps are \(\overline{\alpha^{-1}}=\alpha^{-1}C_U(L_j)\) and \(\overline{\beta^{-1}\sigma_j^{-1}}=\beta^{-1}\sigma_j^{-1}C_U(L_j)\).

Write \(M={}_{\mathbb C[K]}(\mathbb C[G])_{\mathbb C[H]}\) with actions through \(\alpha_*,\beta_*\), and \(M_j=\mathbb C[K]\otimes_{\mathbb C[L_j]}\mathbb C[H]\) with actions in the tensor product through \(\lambda_j,\mu_j\). A boundary with middle region \(G\) represents \(\mathsf D_{\bar\alpha}^{K,G}\otimes_{\mathbb C[G]}\mathsf I_{\bar\beta}^{G,H}\cong M\); one with middle region \(L_j\) represents \(\mathsf I_{\overline{\alpha^{-1}}}^{K,L_j}\otimes_{\mathbb C[L_j]} \mathsf D_{\overline{\beta^{-1}\sigma_j^{-1}}}^{L_j,H}\cong M_j\).

Theorem 2.2 (Generalized Mackey decomposition). There are bimodule maps \(p_j:M\to M_j\) and \(\iota_j:M_j\to M\) such that \[\begin{equation} \mathrm{id}_M=\sum_{j\in J}\iota_j\circ p_j,\qquad p_j\circ\iota_i= \begin{cases} \mathrm{id}_{M_i},&i=j,\\ 0,&i\ne j. \end{cases} \end{equation}\tag{2.3}\] The sum is pointwise finite and gives \[M\cong\bigoplus_{j\in J}M_j,\qquad \operatorname{Res}_{\alpha_*}\,\operatorname{Ind}_{\beta_*} \cong\bigoplus_{j\in J}\operatorname{Ind}_{\lambda_j}\,\operatorname{Res}_{\mu_j}.\]

The inclusion and projection diagrams below extend Khovanov’s finite-group diagrams to conjugate embeddings [5, Prop. 6].

For each \(j\), choose right coset representatives \(h_u\) indexed by \(U_j\), so that \[\begin{equation} H=\bigsqcup_{u\in U_j}({}^{\beta^{-1}\sigma_j^{-1}}\!L_j)h_u . \end{equation}\tag{2.4}\] For \(g\in G\), put \[U'_j(g)=\{u\in U_j: \alpha^{-1}g\beta h_u^{-1}\beta^{-1}\sigma_j^{-1}\alpha\in K\}.\]

Lemma 2.3 (The surviving coset representative). The set \(U'_j(g)\) has exactly one element if \(g\in j\) and is empty otherwise.

Proof. If \(u\in U'_j(g)\), then \(g\beta h_u^{-1}\beta^{-1}\in({}^{\alpha}\!K)\sigma_j\), so \(g\in j\). Conversely, write \(g=\alpha k\alpha^{-1}\sigma_j\beta h\beta^{-1}\) with \(k\in K\) and \(h\in H\). Cancelling \(k\) in the defining test, we obtain \[\begin{aligned} u\in U'_j(g) &\iff \sigma_j\beta h h_u^{-1}\beta^{-1}\sigma_j^{-1}\in{}^{\alpha}\!K\\ &\iff h h_u^{-1}\in{}^{\beta^{-1}\sigma_j^{-1}}\!L_j. \end{aligned}\] The middle expression is already in \({}^{\sigma_j\beta}\!H\). The last condition holds for exactly one \(u\) by (2.4). ◻

Proof of Theorem 2.2. The dot \(\sigma_j\) and the multiplication cap give \[\iota_j(k\otimes h)=\alpha k\alpha^{-1}\sigma_j\beta h\beta^{-1}.\] This formula is balanced because \(\alpha_*(\lambda_j(\ell))=\ell\) and \(\sigma_j\beta_*(\mu_j(\ell))=\ell\sigma_j\) for \(\ell\in L_j\). It commutes with the left \(K\)-action and the right \(H\)-action.

For the other crossing, the reverse cup supplies the coset terms, the dot inserts \(\sigma_j^{-1}\), and the trace \(E_\alpha:\mathbb C[G]\to\mathbb C[K]\) keeps only the indices in \(U'_j(g)\). Thus \[p_j(g)=\sum_{u\in U'_j(g)} \alpha^{-1}g\beta h_u^{-1}\beta^{-1}\sigma_j^{-1}\alpha\otimes h_u .\] Extend this formula linearly. If the coset index in (2.4) is infinite, apply the trace term by term. Lemma 2.3 gives at most one term on each basis element.

Apply \(\iota_j\) to each surviving term. The dot \(\sigma_j\) cancels \(\sigma_j^{-1}\), and \(h_u^{-1}h_u=1\), so \[\begin{equation} (\iota_j\circ p_j)(g)=|U'_j(g)|\,g . \end{equation}\tag{2.5}\] By Lemma 2.3, this fixes \(g\) if \(g\in j\) and sends it to zero otherwise. Each \(g\in G\) lies in exactly one double coset. Summing over \(j\) gives \(\mathrm{id}_M\), and the sum is finite on each input.

In the reverse order, let \(g=\iota_i(k\otimes h)\). Then \(g\in i\), so \(p_j(g)=0\) for \(i\ne j\). For \(i=j\) let \(u\) be the unique element of \(U'_j(g)\). The lemma gives \(\ell=\sigma_j\beta h h_u^{-1}\beta^{-1}\sigma_j^{-1}\in L_j\). Substituting into \(p_j\) and using the tensor relation gives \[\begin{equation} (p_j\circ\iota_j)(k\otimes h) =k\lambda_j(\ell)\otimes h_u =k\otimes\mu_j(\ell)h_u =k\otimes h . \end{equation}\tag{2.6}\]

The two identities make \((p_j)_j\) the inverse of the bimodule map \(\bigoplus_j M_j\to M\) with components \(\iota_j\). Thus each \(p_j\) is a bimodule map. Tensoring the isomorphism on the right gives the stated functor isomorphism. ◻

3 Right curls, trivalent moves, and admissible labellings

We establish local rules for comparing diagrams, including how dots pass through curls or three-strand vertices, and when bending a strand at such a vertex preserves its map.

3.1 Right curls and dots

The right curl on \(\mathsf I_{\bar\alpha}^{G,H}\) uses the reverse cup and multiplication cap for the embedding by \(\delta\) of \(K\) into \(H\). It requires \([H:{}^{\delta}\!K]<\infty\). For the inclusion this index is \([H:K]\).

Proposition 3.1 (Right curl and dot slide). The curl multiplies by \([H:{}^{\delta}\!K]\). For \(d=\alpha\beta^{-1}\in G\), the dot \(r_d(x)=xd\) changes \(\bar\alpha\) to \(\bar\beta\). Using the same embedding of \(K\) into \(H\), the dot below, inside, or above the curl gives \(x\mapsto[H:{}^{\delta}\!K]xd\).

For the four identities below take \(\gamma,\gamma'\) for embeddings of \(H\) into \(G\) and \(\alpha,\beta\) for embeddings of \(K\) into \(H\). Set \(d=\gamma(\gamma')^{-1}\in G\) and \(e=\alpha\beta^{-1}\in H\). Write \(m_{\gamma,\alpha}\) and \(m^\downarrow_{\gamma,\alpha}\) for the induction and restriction merges and \(\ell_a(x)=ax\). The identities are in order for the induction merge, induction split, restriction merge, and restriction split. \[\begin{aligned} m_{\gamma',\beta}\circ(r_d\otimes r_e) &=r_{d\gamma'e(\gamma')^{-1}}\circ m_{\gamma,\alpha},\qquad\text{(3.1)}\\ (r_d\otimes r_e)\circ m_{\gamma,\alpha}^{-1} &=m_{\gamma',\beta}^{-1}\circ r_{d\gamma'e(\gamma')^{-1}},\qquad\text{(3.2)}\\ m^\downarrow_{\gamma',\beta}\circ(\ell_{e^{-1}}\otimes\ell_{d^{-1}}) &=\ell_{\gamma'e^{-1}(\gamma')^{-1}d^{-1}}\circ m^\downarrow_{\gamma,\alpha},\qquad\text{(3.3)}\\ (\ell_{e^{-1}}\otimes\ell_{d^{-1}})\circ(m^\downarrow_{\gamma,\alpha})^{-1} &=(m^\downarrow_{\gamma',\beta})^{-1}\circ \ell_{\gamma'e^{-1}(\gamma')^{-1}d^{-1}} .\qquad\text{(3.4)}\end{aligned}\]

Proof of the induction merge identity. Since \(m_{\gamma,\alpha}(x\otimes y)=x\gamma_*(y)\) and \(d\gamma'_*(y)=\gamma_*(y)d\), we have \[m_{\gamma',\beta}(xd\otimes ye) =xd\,\gamma'_*(ye) =x\gamma_*(y)\,d\gamma'_*(e).\] This gives (3.1). ◻

Proof of the curl identity. Choose \(T\) with \(H=\bigsqcup_{t\in T}({}^{\delta}\!K)t\). The cup gives \(\sum_t x\alpha_*(t^{-1})\otimes t\) in \(\mathbb C[G]\otimes_{\mathbb C[K]}\mathbb C[H]\). The cap gives \(\sum_t x\alpha_*(t^{-1})\alpha_*(t)=[H:{}^{\delta}\!K]x\). A dot inside the curl inserts \(d\) in the first factor and changes the cap to use \(\beta_*\). Since \(\alpha_*(t^{-1})d\,\beta_*(t)=d\), the result is \([H:{}^{\delta}\!K]xd\). This also equals composition with the scalar curl on either side. ◻

More generally, insert on the induction factor the bimodule endomorphism given by right multiplication by \(a\in\mathbb C[H]\) commuting with \(\mathbb C[{}^{\delta}\!K]\). It is an eligible single dot under our convention when each supported group element centralizes \({}^{\delta}\!K\); commuting only after addition suffices for the endomorphism in this calculation. The resulting curl acts by right multiplication by \[\alpha_*\!\left(\sum_{t\in T}t^{-1}at\right).\] This follows by inserting \(a\) between \(t^{-1}\) and \(t\). Replacing \(t\) by \(\ell t\), \(\ell\in{}^{\delta}\!K\), leaves the summand unchanged. Right multiplication permutes the cosets, so the sum is central in \(\mathbb C[H]\). A dot between the \(\alpha_*\) and \(\beta_*\) strands intertwines their images of this sum.

3.2 Moving dots across a merge or split

The diagrams below show the case \(d=1\) of the four identities in Proposition 3.1. Read from bottom to top.

Moving a dot back to a branch.

Fix the incoming labels of a merge. A dot \(D\in G\) on its merged strand can move to the right input exactly when \(D\in{}^{\gamma}\!H\). Necessity follows from (3.1) with \(d=1\). For sufficiency take \(e=\gamma^{-1}D\gamma\in H\) and \(\beta=e^{-1}\alpha\). This embeds \(K\) into \(H\) and gives the same output embedding, since both conjugate \((\gamma\alpha)_*\) by \(D^{-1}\). A dot can always move to the left input by taking \(\gamma'=D^{-1}\gamma\) and \(e=1\).

For a split with left label \(\bar\alpha\), moving a dot to the right output requires \(D\in{}^{\alpha}\!H\). To move it to the left output, factor the given input representative \(b\) as \((b\gamma^{-1})\gamma\). Since \(D=b(\alpha\gamma)^{-1}\), the left representative \(b\gamma^{-1}=D\alpha\) embeds \(H\) into \(G\).

Moving a curl across a vertex.

A right curl with no dot multiplies by its index. It can move across a merge or split if the new curl has the same index. The right input and the merged strand have the same right region \(K\). The left input has right region \(H\), where \[[H:{}^{\alpha}\!L]=[H:{}^{\alpha}\!K]\,[K:L]\] for \(L\le K\) and finite indices.

Example 3.1 (Two curls with different indices). For \(G=H=C_2\) and \(K=L=1\), the curls using \(1\) multiply by \(1\) on the right input and by \(2\) on the left.

Dividing each curl by its finite index gives the identity.

3.3 When one shape can replace another

For shapes \(A,B\) with the same boundary shape, write \(A\preceq B\) if every labelling of \(A\) can be replaced by a labelling of \(B\) with the same boundary groups, boundary cosets, and map. Internal labels may change. This is inclusion of the sets of maps at each fixed boundary, so the relation is reflexive and transitive.

Proposition 3.2. Let \(A\) be the induction merge with a dot on the right input, and let \(B\) be the induction merge with a dot on the merged strand. Then \(A\preceq B\) and \(B\npreceq A\).

Proof. The induction-merge slide in (3.1) is defined for every admissible labelling of \(A\) and preserves its boundary and map.

Example 3.2 proves \(B\npreceq A\). ◻

Example 3.2 (A dot that cannot move to the right input). Take the standard inclusions in the finitary symmetric group \[K=\operatorname{Sym}(\{1,2\})\ \le\ H=\operatorname{Sym}(\{1,2,3\})\ \le\ G=\operatorname{Sym}(\{1,2,3,4\}).\] Fix all boundary strands to be these inclusions and their composite. Put \(g=(3\,4)\) on the merged strand of \(B\). It centralizes \(K\), so \(g,1\) represent the same label there. The map sends \(1\otimes1\) to \(g\). In shape \(A\), the image of \(1\otimes1\) is the right-input label and lies in \(H\), whereas \(g\notin H\).

3.4 Maps between induction bimodules

Proposition 3.3. The space of bimodule maps from \(\mathsf I_{\bar\alpha}^{G,H}\) to \(\mathsf I_{\bar\beta}^{G,H}\) has a basis given by right multiplication by the sums of the finite orbits of \[h\cdot g=\alpha_*(h)g\,\beta_*(h)^{-1} \qquad(h\in H,\ g\in G).\] The single group-element dots are the singleton orbits. If \(H\) is finite, the sum over each orbit is realized by a diagram.

Proof. A left \(\mathbb C[G]\)-linear map is right multiplication by its value \(a\) at \(1\). It respects the \(\mathbb C[H]\)-action precisely when \(\alpha_*(h)a=a\beta_*(h)\) for every \(h\). Invariance forces the coefficients to be constant on an orbit, and finite support excludes infinite orbits. This proves the basis statement.

For finite \(H\), insert the reverse cup for \(1\hookrightarrow H\) on the right of the \(\bar\alpha\)-strand. Merge the two induction strands to an induction from \(1\) to \(G\), put the dot \(g\) on that strand, then split using the \(\bar\beta\)-strand and apply the multiplication cap for \(1\hookrightarrow H\). The resulting value at \(x\) is \[x\sum_{t\in H}\alpha_*(t^{-1})g\beta_*(t) =x\,|\operatorname{Stab}_H(g)|\sum_{z\in H\cdot g}z .\] The stabilizer order is invertible in \(\mathbb C\). ◻

Example 3.3 (A block swap). Let \[B_1=\{1,2,3\},\quad B_2=\{4,5,6\},\quad B_3=\{7,8,9\},\quad B_4=\{10,11,12\}.\] Put \[G=\operatorname{Sym}(B_1\cup B_2)\times\operatorname{Sym}(B_3\cup B_4), \qquad H=\prod_{i=1}^4\operatorname{Sym}(B_i).\] Take \(\alpha=(4\,7)(5\,8)(6\,9)\) and \(\beta=1\). The embeddings send \((4\,5)\) to transpositions in different \(G\)-blocks. Every element of \(G\) preserves the two blocks, so there is no group-element dot between these embeddings. The sum over the orbit of \(1\) gives a nonzero diagram map by Proposition 3.3.

A criterion for group-element dots.

Suppose \(H,G\) are products of symmetric groups on disjoint blocks in the group of finitely supported permutations. Each embedding sends a non-singleton \(H\)-block into a \(G\)-block. A dot exists exactly when the two images of each non-singleton \(H\)-block lie in the same \(G\)-block. Necessity follows by testing transpositions. For sufficiency extend \(\beta(x)\mapsto\alpha(x)\) on those images to permutations in the \(G\)-blocks. These partial bijections differ from the identity at finitely many points, so the extensions can have finite support. Together they give a dot in \(G\).

Example 3.4 (A dot between two images in one block). \(1\) and \((2\,3)\) send \(\{1,2\}\) to different sets in \(S_4\), and \((2\,3)\) gives a dot.

A stronger embedding condition.

Fix a representative \(\beta\) for the embedding of \(K\) into \(H\). Suppose \({}^{\sigma\beta^{-1}}\!H\le G\) whenever \({}^{\sigma}\!K\le G\). A reverse slide onto the right leg can still fail.

Example 3.5 (A reverse slide under the stronger embedding condition). Use \(K=\prod_i\operatorname{Sym}(B_i)\), \[H=\operatorname{Sym}(B_1\cup B_2)\times\operatorname{Sym}(B_3\cup B_4)\], \(G=S_{\{1,\ldots,12\}}\), and \(\beta=1\). If \({}^{\sigma}\!K\le G\), the support of \({}^{\sigma}\!K\) forces \(\sigma\) to preserve \(\{1,\ldots,12\}\), so \({}^{\sigma}\!H\le G\). The block swap \(\alpha=(4\,7)(5\,8)(6\,9)\) conjugates \(K\) to itself and lies in \(G\setminus H\). It labels a dot on the merged strand from the inclusion to \(\bar\alpha\). It cannot move to the right input because it lies outside \(H\).

Bounds on the support of representatives.

For a finite subgroup \(X\) given by symmetric groups on disjoint blocks, let \(\operatorname{supp}X\) be the union of its non-singleton blocks and \(\widehat X=\operatorname{Sym}(\operatorname{supp}X)\). The conditions \(\alpha\in G\widehat H\) and \(\beta\in H\widehat K\) do not ensure \(\alpha\beta\in G\widehat K\).

Example 3.6 (Support bounds under composition). Take \[G=H=\operatorname{Sym}(\{1,\ldots,6\}) \times\operatorname{Sym}(\{7,\ldots,12\}), \qquad K=\operatorname{Sym}(\{1,2,3\}).\] Let \(\alpha\) swap the two six-point blocks and let \(\beta=1\). The two embeddings satisfy the support bounds. Every element of \(G\widehat K\) preserves each six-point block, whereas \(\alpha\beta\) carries the support of \(K\) into the second block. Even the composite strand label has no representative in \(G\widehat K\).

Example 3.7 (Support bounds for an inverse product). Consider whether \({}^{\beta\alpha^{-1}}\!H\le G\) when \(\alpha,\beta\in\widehat G H\) both embed \(H\) in \(G\). Take \(G\) to be the product of the symmetric groups on \(\{1,2\},\{3,4\},\{5,6\}\), \(H=\operatorname{Sym}(\{1,2\})\), \(\alpha=(1\,3)(2\,4)\), and \(\beta=(4\,5)\). Both permutations belong to \(\widehat G H\) and embed \(H\) in \(G\). But \(\beta\alpha^{-1}\) sends \(\{1,2\}\) to \(\{3,5\}\), so \({}^{\beta\alpha^{-1}}\!H\nleq G\).

3.5 Bends at a trivalent vertex

Proposition 3.4 (Bend identities). Let \(K\xrightarrow{\alpha_*}H\xrightarrow{\gamma_*}G\) be conjugation embeddings. The two diagrams in each pair below give the same bimodule map. The multiplication-cap and ordinary-cup identities hold at all indices. The trace-cap identity holds when \([H:{}^{\alpha}\!K]<\infty\), and the reverse-cup identity holds when \([G:{}^{\gamma\alpha}\!K]<\infty\).

Proof. Read the diagrams from bottom to top and evaluate on group elements. Use the merges, splits, and trace caps \(E_\delta\) of Section 1.2.

Multiplication cap. For \(g\otimes h\in\mathbb C[G]\otimes_{\mathbb C[K]}\mathbb C[H]\), we obtain \[\begin{aligned} \text{LHS}\quad g\otimes h &\longmapsto g\otimes1\otimes h \longmapsto g\otimes h \longmapsto g\gamma_*(h),\\ \text{RHS}\quad g\otimes h &\longmapsto g\otimes h\otimes1\otimes1 \longmapsto g\otimes\gamma_*(h)\otimes1 \longmapsto g\gamma_*(h). \end{aligned}\] The left cap multiplies in \(H\), then balancing over \(\mathbb C[H]\) identifies \(g\otimes h\) with \(g\gamma_*(h)\). On the right, the ordinary cup inserts \(1\otimes1\), and the cap multiplies in \(G\).

Trace cap. Choose \(h_i\in H\) with \(H=\bigsqcup_{i\in I}(\alpha K\alpha^{-1})h_i\). On \(g\otimes g'\in\mathbb C[G]\otimes_{\mathbb C[G]}\mathbb C[G]\), the left side is \[\text{LHS}\quad g\otimes g' \longmapsto 1\otimes g\otimes g' \longmapsto 1\otimes E_\gamma(gg') \longmapsto E_\gamma(gg').\] The reverse cup on the right inserts \(\sum_i h_i^{-1}\otimes h_i\). \[\begin{aligned} \text{RHS}\quad g\otimes g' &\longmapsto \sum_i g\otimes g'\otimes h_i^{-1}\otimes h_i\\ &\longmapsto \sum_i g\otimes g'\gamma_*(h_i^{-1})\otimes h_i\\ &\longmapsto \sum_i \alpha_*\!\left(E_{\gamma\alpha} (gg'\gamma_*(h_i^{-1}))\right)h_i. \end{aligned}\] If \(gg'\notin\gamma H\gamma^{-1}\), all terms vanish. If \(gg'=\gamma_*(h)\), only the \(i\) with \(hh_i^{-1}\in\alpha K\alpha^{-1}\) survives. Its term is \((hh_i^{-1})h_i=h=E_\gamma(gg')\).

Ordinary cup. For \(h\in\mathbb C[H]\), the two outputs are \[\begin{aligned} \text{LHS}\quad h &\longmapsto 1\otimes1\otimes h \longmapsto 1\otimes\gamma_*(h),\\ \text{RHS}\quad h &\longmapsto h\otimes1\otimes1 \longmapsto \gamma_*(h)\otimes1. \end{aligned}\] They are equal by balancing over \(\mathbb C[G]\).

Reverse cup. Use the same \(h_i\) as in the trace cap. On the left, \[\text{LHS}\quad g \longmapsto \sum_i g\otimes h_i^{-1}\otimes h_i \longmapsto \sum_i g\gamma_*(h_i^{-1})\otimes h_i.\] Choose \(g_j\) with \(G=\bigsqcup_j(\gamma H\gamma^{-1})g_j\) and \(g_0=1\). Then \(\gamma_*(h_i)g_j\) are representatives for the cosets \(((\gamma\alpha)K(\gamma\alpha)^{-1})\gamma_*(h_i)g_j\). On the right, write the input as \(g\otimes1\) over \(\mathbb C[G]\) before applying the large cup. The cup, split, and trace give \[\begin{aligned} \text{RHS}\quad g &\longmapsto \sum_{i,j} g(\gamma_*(h_i)g_j)^{-1}\otimes\gamma_*(h_i)g_j\otimes1\\ &\longmapsto \sum_{i,j} g(\gamma_*(h_i)g_j)^{-1}\otimes1\otimes\gamma_*(h_i)g_j\otimes1\\ &\longmapsto \sum_{i,j} g(\gamma_*(h_i)g_j)^{-1}\otimes E_\gamma(\gamma_*(h_i)g_j)\\ &=\sum_i g\gamma_*(h_i^{-1})\otimes h_i. \end{aligned}\] Only \(j=0\) survives the trace. The finite index for the large cup also makes the smaller cup on the left finite. ◻

The equations let us draw the vertices with three straight segments.

4 Reidemeister I move

We evaluate each of the four oriented curls and identify the two that multiply the identity by a subgroup index.

Use \(K\xrightarrow{\delta_*}H\xrightarrow{\alpha_*}G\). The subscript on \(\mathrm{id}\) lists the groups on the left and right.

Theorem 4.1 (Reidemeister I). The four curls shown below satisfy \[\begin{aligned} \text{right curl on an up-strand} &= [H:{}^{\delta}\!K]\,\mathrm{id}_{G\cdot H},\qquad\text{(4.1)}\\ \text{right curl on a down-strand} &= \mathrm{id}_{K\cdot H},\qquad\text{(4.2)}\\ \text{left curl on an up-strand} &= \mathrm{id}_{H\cdot K},\qquad\text{(4.3)}\\ \text{left curl on a down-strand} &= [H:{}^{\delta}\!K]\,\mathrm{id}_{H\cdot G}.\qquad\text{(4.4)}\end{aligned}\] The \(K\)-loops require finite \([H:{}^{\delta}\!K]\). The \(G\)-loops hold at arbitrary index.

Proof. For the loops enclosing \(K\), choose \(T\subseteq H\) with \(H=\bigsqcup_{t\in T}({}^{\delta}\!K)t\). The reverse cup inserts \(\sum_t t^{-1}\otimes t\). Apply \(\alpha_*\) to each factor before multiplying. \[\begin{aligned} \text{right curl, up}\quad &x\longmapsto \sum\nolimits_{t\in T}x\,\alpha_*(t^{-1})\alpha_*(t) =[H:{}^{\delta}\!K]\,x,\\ \text{left curl, down}\quad &x\longmapsto \sum\nolimits_{t\in T}\alpha_*(t^{-1})\alpha_*(t)\,x =[H:{}^{\delta}\!K]\,x . \end{aligned}\]

For the loops enclosing \(G\), the ordinary unit inserts \(1\otimes1\). The join uses the composite embedding, and the cap applies \(E_\alpha\) to the product. \[\begin{aligned} \text{right curl, down}\quad &x\longmapsto \alpha_*(x)\otimes1\longmapsto E_\alpha(\alpha_*(x))=x,\\ \text{left curl, up}\quad &x\longmapsto 1\otimes\alpha_*(x)\longmapsto E_\alpha(\alpha_*(x))=x . \end{aligned}\] Here \(E_\alpha\alpha_*=\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb C[H]}\). Tensoring gives the functor identities. ◻

Over \(\mathbb C\), dividing either \(K\)-loop by its finite index \([H:{}^{\delta}\!K]\) gives the identity.

5 Reidemeister II move

We evaluate pairs of crossings. For strands pointing in opposite directions, the identities come from Mackey decomposition. For parallel strands, merging reduces the composite to multiplication by a single element of the group algebra.

5.1 Mixed orientations and Mackey decomposition

Use \(J,\sigma_j,L_j,M,M_j\) from Section 2.2. The two boundary strands of \(M\) point in opposite directions, with \(G\) between them. To allow a different middle group, take \({}^{a}\!L\le K\), \({}^{b}\!L\le H\) and \(s=\alpha a(\beta b)^{-1}\in G\). Use \(s,s^{-1}\) as the two crossing labels and put \[M_L=\mathbb C[K]\otimes_{\mathbb C[L]}\mathbb C[H],\qquad L_s=({}^{\alpha}\!K)\cap({}^{s\beta}\!H),\qquad n=[L_s:{}^{\alpha a}\!L].\] The tensor product has basis \(K\times_L H\), the classes \([k,h]\) of pairs under \([ka\ell a^{-1},h]=[k,b\ell b^{-1}h]\), \(\ell\in L\). The relation identifies exactly the pairs equated by balancing the tensor product.

Proposition 5.1 (Two crossings with opposite orientations). Suppose \(n<\infty\). Let \(p_L:M\to M_L\) and \(\iota_L:M_L\to M\) be the two crossings. Write \(q([k,h])=\alpha k\alpha^{-1}s\beta h\beta^{-1}\). If \(s\in j\), then \[\iota_L\circ p_L=nP_j,\qquad p_L\circ\iota_L(z)=\sum_{q(w)=q(z)}w \quad(z\in K\times_L H),\] where \(P_j\) fixes the basis elements in \(j\) and sends the others to zero. The second composite is the identity exactly when \(n=1\). For \(n>1\), no scalar multiple of it is the identity.

Proof. Since \(s\beta b=\alpha a\), conjugation by \(s\) identifies the two images of \(L\). Thus \({}^{\alpha a}\!L\le L_s\) and \(q\) respects the relation on pairs. The dot \(s\) and multiplication cap give \(\iota_L([k,h])=q([k,h])\).

Choose \(T\) with \(H=\bigsqcup_{t\in T}({}^{b}\!L)t\). The reverse cup supplies \(t^{-1}\otimes t\), the dot multiplies the first factor by \(s^{-1}\), and the trace \(E_\alpha\) gives \[p_L(g)=\sum_{t\in T} E_\alpha\bigl(g\beta t^{-1}\beta^{-1}s^{-1}\bigr)\otimes t .\] Each class has a unique representative \((k,t)\) with \(t\in T\). The trace returns \(k\) exactly when \(q([k,t])=g\). Hence \(p_L(g)=\sum_{q(w)=g}w\).

The action of \(K\times H\) is \((k_0,h_0)[k,h]=[k_0k,hh_0^{-1}]\) and \((k_0,h_0)g=\alpha k_0\alpha^{-1}g\beta h_0^{-1}\beta^{-1}\). The balanced set is transitive and \(q\) is equivariant with image \(j\). Sending \((k_0,h_0)\) to \(\alpha k_0\alpha^{-1}\) identifies the stabilizers of \(s\) and \([1,1]\) with \(L_s\) and \({}^{\alpha a}\!L\), respectively. Their index gives \(n\) preimages for each \(g\in j\). The sums are therefore finite and define a bimodule map. Applying \(q\) to the sum gives \(ng\) on \(j\). In the other order the result sums all preimages of \(q(z)\). If \(n>1\), this includes a basis element besides \(z\). ◻

The reverse cup is a map when \([H:{}^{b}\!L]<\infty\). At infinite index the formula for \(p_L\) sums only the terms that survive the trace. It still defines a map for finite \(n\). For infinite \(n\), the sum on \(g\in j\) has infinite support.

Mackey cancellation.

Take \(L=L_j\), \(a=\alpha^{-1}\), \(b=\beta^{-1}\sigma_j^{-1}\). Then \(s=\sigma_j\), \(n=1\), and the two orders give \[p_j\circ\iota_i= \begin{cases} \mathrm{id}_{M_i},&i=j,\\ 0,&i\ne j, \end{cases} \qquad \sum_{j\in J}\iota_j\circ p_j=\mathrm{id}_M .\] Here \(q\) is a bijection onto \(j\). For \(i\ne j\), \(p_j\) vanishes on the image of \(\iota_i\). The projections sum to the identity because the double cosets partition \(G\). Read the diagrams from bottom to top.

Lemma 5.2 (Transport to the Mackey representative). Let \(s=\alpha a(\beta b)^{-1}\in j\) and write \[s=(\alpha k\alpha^{-1})\sigma_j(\beta h\beta^{-1}), \qquad k\in K,\quad h\in H.\] Then \(L'={}^{\alpha k^{-1}a}\!L\le L_j\). Use the restrictions of \(\lambda_j,\mu_j\) for the embeddings of \(L'\). The map \[[x,y]_L\longmapsto[xk,hy]_{L'}\] is a bijection respecting the left \(K\)-action and right \(H\)-action. Its linear extension intertwines the multiplication crossings with labels \(s,\sigma_j\), and the projection crossings with labels \(s^{-1},\sigma_j^{-1}\) when their fiber sums are finite. Moreover, \([L_j:L']=[L_s:{}^{\alpha a}\!L]\).

Proof. Multiplying the factorization of \(s\) by \(\beta b\) gives \(t=\alpha k^{-1}a=\sigma_j\beta hb\). The first expression puts \(tLt^{-1}\) inside \({}^{\alpha}\!K\) and the second puts it inside \({}^{\sigma_j\beta}\!H\). Hence \(L'=tLt^{-1}\le L_j\). For \(\ell\in L\) the restricted embeddings send \(t\ell t^{-1}\) to \[\lambda_j(t\ell t^{-1})=k^{-1}a\ell a^{-1}k,\qquad \mu_j(t\ell t^{-1})=hb\ell b^{-1}h^{-1}.\] These identities carry the balancing relation for \(L\) to the one for \(L'\) and back under the inverse shifts. The shifts commute with the left \(K\)-action and right \(H\)-action. The crossing values agree by \[\alpha(xk)\alpha^{-1}\sigma_j\beta(hy)\beta^{-1} =\alpha x\alpha^{-1}s\beta y\beta^{-1}.\] The bijection also identifies the fibers, so it intertwines the reverse crossings when the fiber sums are finite. Finally, conjugation by \(\alpha k^{-1}\alpha^{-1}\) takes \(L_s\) to \(L_j\) and \({}^{\alpha a}\!L\) to \(L'\), proving the index equality. ◻

This justifies restricting the Mackey embeddings in the calculation below. For any admissible dot in \(j\), the composite on \(\mathbb C[G]\) is the projection onto \(j\) multiplied by the transported index, whenever the reverse crossing is defined.

Smaller internal groups and intersections.

Restrict the embeddings of \(L_j\) to \(L\le L_j\), and write \(p_{L,j},\iota_{L,j}\) for the resulting crossings. For finite \([L_j:L]\), Proposition 5.1 gives \(\iota_{L,j}\circ p_{L,j}=[L_j:L]\,(\iota_j\circ p_j)\).

In the rightmost diagram, \(q\) maps balanced pairs to the double coset \(j\). \[q:K\times_L H\longrightarrow j,\qquad q([k,h]_L)=\alpha k\alpha^{-1}\sigma_j\beta h\beta^{-1}.\] The lower crossing \(\iota_{L,j}\) is the linear extension of \(q\). The upper crossing \(p_{L,j}\) sends \(q(z)\) to the sum of its \([L_j:L]\) preimages, so \(p_{L,j}\circ\iota_{L,j}\) is the identity exactly when \(L=L_j\).

For nonempty \(J_0\subseteq J\), the group \(L=\bigcap_{j\in J_0}L_j\) can be used separately for each \(j\in J_0\). Use \(\alpha^{-1}\) into \(K\) and \(\beta^{-1}\sigma_j^{-1}\) into \(H\). The right labels for \(i,j\) agree exactly when \(\sigma_i\sigma_j^{-1}\in C_U(L)\). If all \([L_j:L]\) are finite, termwise division gives \[\sum_{j\in J_0}\frac{\iota_{L,j}\circ p_{L,j}}{[L_j:L]} = \sum_{j\in J_0}\iota_j\circ p_j .\] Also \(p_{L,j}\circ\iota_{L,i}=0\) for \(i\ne j\), since the double cosets are disjoint. For infinite \(J_0\) the sum of maps is pointwise finite, with one term on each basis element in the selected cosets. A finite \(J_0\) gives a sum of diagrams when the cups exist.

Example 5.1 (Two Mackey summands with the same internal labels). For \(G=S_3\times C_2\), use the inclusions of \(K=H=\langle(12)\rangle\times C_2\). The double cosets have representatives \(1,((13),1)\), with \(L_1=K\) and \(L_2=\{1\}\times C_2\). Their common group \(L=L_2\) is central, so both pairs use the same internal strand labels. The first pair has index \(2\), the second index \(1\).

Proposition 5.3 (A single pair of crossings). A composite \(M\to M_L\to M\) cannot project onto two or more double cosets, even with arbitrary bimodule maps at the crossings.

Proof. For a \(K\times H\)-module \(V\), quotient by the span of \(gv-v\) for \(g\in K\times H\), \(v\in V\). Equivariant maps descend to these quotients. For a permutation module the quotient has one basis vector per orbit. The quotient for \(M_L\) has dimension one because the action is transitive. Thus any map through \(M_L\) has rank at most one on the quotients. The projection onto several double cosets has one independent image vector for each selected coset. ◻

A single pair can give \(\mathrm{id}_M\) only when \(|J|=1\). In that case use the full Mackey intersection, with the same convention for infinite index as above.

5.2 Strands pointing the same way

The bottom, middle, and top chains through \(H,B,H'\) have composite conjugators \(u=\alpha\beta\), \(v=\gamma\delta\), and \(w=\alpha'\beta'\).

Theorem 5.4 (Parallel Reidemeister II). Two induction crossings labelled \(d,e\), read bottom to top, equal a merge, a dot \(de\) from \(\bar u\) to \(\bar w\), and a split. For \(de=1\) the merged labels agree and the dot disappears. The remaining map is the identity if both boundary sequences agree. Each group-element label \(d\) has an admissible return label \(d^{-1}\).

Proof. The three merges identify the tensor products with copies of \(\mathbb C[G]\), with right actions through \(u_*,v_*,w_*\). The split and merge on the middle product cancel, leaving \(x\mapsto xd\mapsto xde\). For group-element dots \(u^{-1}dv\) and \(v^{-1}ew\) centralize \(K\), so their product \(u^{-1}de\,w\) does too. The same test applies to each product of terms in linear labels. If \(de=1\), then \(u^{-1}w\in C_U(K)\), so the merged strand stays the same through the dot. If the boundaries agree, the top split inverts the bottom merge. Finally \(v^{-1}d^{-1}u=(u^{-1}dv)^{-1}\) proves admissibility for the return dot. Both products of \(d,d^{-1}\) equal \(1\). ◻

For two restriction strands, the group-element labels act on the left, giving \(x\mapsto d^{-1}x\mapsto e^{-1}d^{-1}x=(de)^{-1}x\). The product label is again \(de\), including for linear labels by expansion in group elements.

6 Reidemeister III move

With the boundary fixed, we determine when one three-crossing shape can replace the other. We treat three induction strands and the six directions of replacement with one restriction strand, including cases that require a sum or admit no replacement.

6.1 Three induction strands

For the standard inclusions \(S_n\subset S_{n+1}\), Khovanov obtains the three-upward-strand braid relation from the relation for adjacent transpositions [5, Prop. 7].

All strands point up. For adjacent groups \(X,Y\le U\), \(\boxed{XY}\) represents the label \(\boxed{XY}C_U(Y)\). Fix the six outer groups and the six boundary strand labels shown below.

Diagrams and their classes.

Let \(\mathcal L,\mathcal R\) be the admissible labellings of the left and right shapes at this boundary. A labelling specifies the middle group, the three interior strand cosets, and the three crossing dots. Each crossing merges, applies its dot, and splits. A dot can be a finite \(\mathbb C\)-linear combination of the group elements allowed between the two merged strand labels.

Write \(\operatorname{ev}(\mathsf D)\) for the resulting map from the bottom tensor product to the top one. Within each shape let \(\mathsf D\sim\mathsf E\) mean \(\operatorname{ev}(\mathsf D)=\operatorname{ev}(\mathsf E)\), and write \([\mathsf D]\) for the equivalence class of \(\mathsf D\).

Multipliers.

Choose boundary representatives and put \[u=\boxed{GA}\boxed{AD}\boxed{DF},\qquad v=\boxed{GC}\boxed{CE}\boxed{EF}.\] Merging the bottom and top products gives \(\mathbb C[G]\) with right \(\mathbb C[F]\)-actions through \(u_*,v_*\). Every left \(\mathbb C[G]\)-linear map is right multiplication by its value at \(1\), denoted \(m(\mathsf D)\). At the fixed boundary, equality of these values is equality of maps.

Lemma 6.1 (Multipliers under relabelling). At the fixed boundary there are constructions \(\phi:\mathcal L\to\mathcal R\) and \(\psi:\mathcal R\to\mathcal L\) preserving the multiplier of every input diagram. Their two round trips satisfy \[m(\psi(\phi(\mathsf D)))=m(\mathsf D)\quad(\mathsf D\in\mathcal L), \qquad m(\phi(\psi(\mathsf D)))=m(\mathsf D)\quad(\mathsf D\in\mathcal R).\]

Proof. For a single \(p\in G\), the right \(\mathbb C[F]\)-linearity condition is \[u f u^{-1}p=p v f v^{-1}\quad(f\in F) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad u^{-1}pv\in C_U(F).\] At a crossing of the left pair, let \(\lambda_*,\mu_*\) be the incoming and outgoing merged embeddings into \(G\), and let \(d\) be the dot. Merging with the third strand after applying the dot gives \(zd\mu_*(w)=z\lambda_*(w)d\), so the multiplier is \(d\). At a crossing of the right pair, the left embedding \(\gamma_*\) sends the dot to \(\gamma_*(d)\) because \(z\gamma_*(wd)=z\gamma_*(w)\gamma_*(d)\). Hence the multipliers are \[\begin{aligned} \text{left}\quad &x_1(\boxed{GB})_*(x_2)x_3,\\ \text{right}\quad &(\boxed{GA})_*(y_1)y_2(\boxed{GC})_*(y_3). \end{aligned}\] Expand the three labels into group elements. Each triple gives a bimodule map whose multiplier is a single group element \(p\). Thus \(u^{-1}pv\in C_U(F)\) for every \(p\) in the support of \(m\), the group elements with nonzero coefficient.

Construction of \(\phi\).

Given a left-hand diagram with multiplier \(m\), choose the right-hand internal group \(B'=F\) and take \[\boxed{B'F}=1,\qquad \boxed{AB'}=\boxed{AD}\boxed{DF},\qquad \boxed{CB'}=\boxed{CE}\boxed{EF}.\] The outer vertices compare equal embeddings into \(A\) and \(C\), so take \(y_1=y_3=1\). At the middle vertex, \(pv\) represents \(\bar u\) for every \(p\) in the support of \(m\), since \(u^{-1}pv\in C_U(F)\). Using \(v\) for the outgoing label gives the dot \((pv)v^{-1}=p\). Thus \(y_2=m\) is allowed and gives multiplier \(m\).

Construction of \(\psi\).

Given a right-hand diagram with multiplier \(m\), choose the left-hand internal group \(B=G\) and take \[\boxed{GB}=1,\qquad \boxed{BD}=\boxed{GA}\boxed{AD},\qquad \boxed{BE}=\boxed{GC}\boxed{CE}.\] The outer vertices compare equal embeddings of \(D\) and \(E\) into \(G\), so take \(x_1=x_3=1\). The middle vertex compares \(u_*,v_*\) into \(B=G\). The same representatives \(pv,v\) allow \(x_2=m\). The left strand is the inclusion \(G\to G\), so the multiplier is \(m\).

Round-trip calculations.

After the first step the multiplier is still \(m(\mathsf D)\). Returning to the left gives \(B=G\) and dots \((1,m(\mathsf D),1)\). Returning to the right gives \(B'=F\) and the same dot triple. Substitution gives \[\begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} m(\psi(\phi(\mathsf D)))&=1\cdot m(\mathsf D)\cdot1 =m(\mathsf D) &&(\mathsf D\in\mathcal L),\\ m(\phi(\psi(\mathsf D)))&=(\boxed{GA})_*(1)\,m(\mathsf D)\,(\boxed{GC})_*(1) =m(\mathsf D) &&(\mathsf D\in\mathcal R). \end{aligned} \qedhere \end{equation*}\] ◻

Theorem 6.2 (Reidemeister III). At the fixed boundary, the constructions of Lemma 6.1 induce mutually inverse bijections \[\mathcal L/{\sim}\longrightarrow\mathcal R/{\sim}, \quad [\mathsf D]\longmapsto[\phi(\mathsf D)], \qquad \mathcal R/{\sim}\longrightarrow\mathcal L/{\sim}, \quad [\mathsf D]\longmapsto[\psi(\mathsf D)].\] The induced maps are independent of the internal labellings chosen in any construction preserving the boundary and evaluated map.

Proof. If \(\mathsf D\sim\mathsf E\) on the left, the lemma gives \[m(\phi(\mathsf D))=m(\mathsf D)=m(\mathsf E)=m(\phi(\mathsf E))\], so the outputs are equivalent. The same argument applies to \(\psi\). Any other output with the same shape, boundary, and multiplier gives the same class. The two round-trip equalities in the lemma therefore give the identity on each set of classes. ◻

Example 6.1 (A round trip with different labels). Take every group to be \(C_2=\{1,g\}\) and every strand to carry the identity embedding. The left diagram \(\mathsf D\) with dots \((g,1,g)\) has multiplier \(g^2=1\). Its round trip \(\mathsf D'=\psi(\phi(\mathsf D))\) has dots \((1,1,1)\). Both diagrams give the identity bimodule map. Since \(\phi\) uses only the multiplier at this boundary, \(\phi(\mathsf D)=\phi(\mathsf D')\). One return rule cannot recover both inputs. Starting on the right gives the same obstruction for \(\psi\).

Other choices of internal group.

For \(\psi\) one may use any \(B\le G\) containing \({}^{\boxed{GA}}\!A\), \({}^{\boxed{GC}}\!C\), and all group elements with nonzero coefficient in \(m\). The outer embeddings still agree and the inclusion sends \(m\in \mathbb C[B]\) to the same multiplier.

For \(\phi\) with group-element labels, choose representatives at each crossing. A representative \(\alpha\beta c\) of a composite coset factors as \(\alpha(\beta c)\), so the choices can be made on the strands themselves. This gives \[\begin{aligned} x_1&=\boxed{GA}\boxed{AD}\boxed{BD}^{-1}\boxed{GB}^{-1},\\ x_2&=\boxed{BD}'\boxed{DF}\boxed{EF}^{-1}\boxed{BE}^{-1},\\ x_3&=\boxed{GB}'\boxed{BE}'\boxed{CE}^{-1}\boxed{GC}^{-1}. \end{aligned}\] Primes denote other representatives of the same internal labels. Each boundary strand meets one vertex, so use its chosen representative in \(u,v\). Both \(\boxed{GB}\) and \(\boxed{GB}'\) act the same way on \(B\). For \(p=x_1\boxed{GB}x_2\boxed{GB}^{-1}x_3\), cancellation gives \[\begin{aligned} u^{-1}pv={}& \bigl(\boxed{DF}^{-1}\boxed{BD}^{-1}\boxed{BD}'\boxed{DF}\bigr)\\ &{}\cdot \bigl(\boxed{EF}^{-1}\boxed{BE}^{-1}\boxed{GB}^{-1} \boxed{GB}'\boxed{BE}'\boxed{EF}\bigr). \end{aligned}\] The first factor centralizes \({}^{\boxed{DF}^{-1}}\!D\). In the second, \(\boxed{GB}^{-1}\boxed{GB}'\) centralizes \(B\), which contains \({}^{\boxed{BE}}\!E\), and \(\boxed{BE}^{-1}\boxed{BE}'\) centralizes \(E\). Thus the second factor centralizes \({}^{\boxed{EF}^{-1}}\!E\). Both factors centralize \[B''={}^{\boxed{DF}^{-1}}\!D\cap{}^{\boxed{EF}^{-1}}\!E.\] It contains \(F\). Take \(B'={}^{\tau}\!B''\) for any \(\tau\in U\) and use \[\boxed{B'F}=\tau,\qquad \boxed{AB'}=\boxed{AD}\boxed{DF}\tau^{-1},\qquad \boxed{CB'}=\boxed{CE}\boxed{EF}\tau^{-1}.\] The inclusions in the definition of \(B''\) and \(F\le B''\) give these three embeddings. The outer composites agree, so \(y_1=y_3=1\). At the middle vertex \((u\tau^{-1})^{-1}p(v\tau^{-1})=\tau(u^{-1}pv)\tau^{-1}\) centralizes \(B'\), so \(y_2=p\) gives the original multiplier.

6.2 One restriction strand on the outside

Number the three positions from left to right at the bottom. Read the diagrams from bottom to top. The crossing \(s_1\) exchanges positions \(1\) and \(2\), and \(s_2\) exchanges positions \(2\) and \(3\).

In each pair below, the left shape is \(s_1s_2s_1\) and the right shape is \(s_2s_1s_2\). The numbers identify the direction of replacement. Write \(ad=a\circ d\). Write \(\operatorname{Ad}_p\) for conjugation by \(p\).

A label \(s\) on a mixed crossing denotes the parameter in \(q_s([k,h])=\alpha(k)s\beta(h)\). The multiplication crossing is \(q_s\). 6.2.1 Cases 1 and 2. Both mixed crossings are projections

The lower embeddings are \(a:G\to A\), \(b:D\to A\), and \(c:F\to D\). The upper ones are \(d:C\to G\), \(e:E\to C\), and \(f:E\to F\). After merging parallel strands, the bottom basis is \(A\), with actions through \(a,bc\). The top basis is \(G\times_E F\), balanced using \(de,f\).

Lemma 6.3 (Evaluation for two projections). With a group element at each vertex, either shape evaluates to \(Q_p^!\), the sum over the fibers of \[Q_p([g,h])=a(g)p(bc)(h),\qquad [g,h]\in G\times_E F,\] for a unique \(p\in A\). Its admissibility condition and fiber size over its image are \[\begin{equation} \begin{gathered} (ade)(v)p=p(bcf)(v)\qquad(v\in E),\\ n_p=[a(G)\cap p(bc)(F)p^{-1}:(ade)(E)]<\infty . \end{gathered} \end{equation}\tag{6.1}\] A diagram with linear labels evaluates to a finite linear combination of these \(Q_p^!\). Its coefficients are determined by the evaluated map.

Proof. On the left, write \(r:B\to G\), \(s:B\to D\), and \(t:E\to B\). Use \(x,y\) for the first two mixed crossing elements and \(z\) for the upper induction dot. Following the underlying set maps backwards gives \[\begin{aligned} {[g,h]}&\longmapsto[gz^{-1},h]\longmapsto[gz^{-1},y c(h)]\\ &\longmapsto a(gz^{-1})x\,b(yc(h)). \end{aligned}\] Thus \(p=a(z^{-1})x b(y)\). On the right use \(u:B'\to A\), \(v:C\to B'\), and \(w:F\to B'\), with induction dot \(\ell\) and mixed crossing elements \(m,n\). The set maps give \[\begin{aligned} {[g,h]}&\longmapsto[g,nw(h)]\longmapsto a(g)m\,u(nw(h))\\ &\longmapsto a(g)m\,u(nw(h))\ell^{-1}. \end{aligned}\] Thus \(p=m u(n)\ell^{-1}\). Taking inverse images twice gives the fiber of the composite. These fibers are finite because each projection has finite fibers. Balancing gives the first condition in (6.1). The stabilizer index calculation in Proposition 5.1 gives \(n_p\). Expanding the three labels proves the linear formula. The coefficient of \([1,1]\) in \(Q_p^!(z)\) is \(1\) for \(z=p\) and \(0\) otherwise, so the coefficient of each \(Q_p^!\) can be recovered. ◻

Theorem 6.4 (Replacement for two projections). For \(p\) satisfying (6.1), put \(P_p=\langle (ad)(C),p(bc)(F)p^{-1}\rangle\le A\).

  1. In Case 1, a finite sum of right diagrams gives the same map as the left diagram exactly when each \(p\) with nonzero coefficient satisfies \[\begin{equation} [a(G)\cap P_p:(ad)(C)]<\infty. \end{equation}\tag{6.2}\] Each such \(Q_p^!\) has a single right diagram. If \([G:d(C)]<\infty\), one right diagram suffices for the whole sum.

  2. In Case 2, every right diagram has a replacement by a finite sum of left diagrams. If \([G:(de)(E)]<\infty\), one left diagram suffices.

Under \([G:(de)(E)]<\infty\), in particular for finite groups, the single-diagram replacements induce inverse bijections on the two sets of classes modulo equality of evaluated maps.

Proof. For Case 1, the three vertex conditions on the right are \[\begin{gathered} bc=\operatorname{Ad}_{\ell}\circ uw,\qquad ad=\operatorname{Ad}_{m}\circ uv,\\ ve=\operatorname{Ad}_{n}\circ wf . \end{gathered}\] By \(p=m u(n)\ell^{-1}\), the group \(m u(B')m^{-1}\) contains \(P_p\). Its intersection with \(a(G)\) has finite index over \((ad)(C)\), since that index is the middle fiber size. This proves necessity for each term. The coefficient test in Lemma 6.3 gives necessity for finite sums.

For sufficiency take \(B'=P_p\), \(u\) the inclusion, \(v=ad\), \(w=\operatorname{Ad}_p\circ bc\), and \((\ell,m,n)=(p^{-1},1,1)\). The first vertex satisfies \(bc=\operatorname{Ad}_{p^{-1}}\circ uw\). The middle fiber is finite by (6.2). The upper vertex uses \(ve=wf\), and its index is at most \(n_p\) because \((ad)(C)\le a(G)\). If \([G:d(C)]\) is finite, take a common \(B'=A,\ u=\mathrm{id},\ v=ad,\ w=bc\). Put the linear combination of crossing elements on the upper mixed crossing and use \(1\) at the first two. The middle fiber then has size \([G:d(C)]\), and the upper fiber of each crossing element has size at most \(n_p\).

For Case 2 use the full intersection at the first crossing. \[\begin{gathered} B=a^{-1}\bigl(a(G)\cap p b(D)p^{-1}\bigr),\qquad r=\mathrm{incl},\\ s=b^{-1}\circ\operatorname{Ad}_{p^{-1}}\circ a\bigm|_B,\qquad t=de. \end{gathered}\] Use \((x,y,z)=(p,1,1)\). Equation (6.1) gives \(st=cf\). The first fiber has size \(1\) on its image. Transporting the second intersection by \(\operatorname{Ad}_p\circ b\) gives the index \(n_p\). This produces one left diagram per crossing element. If \([G:(de)(E)]<\infty\), take a common \(B=E,\ r=de,\ s=cf,\ t=\mathrm{id}\) and put the linear combination of crossing elements on the lower mixed crossing. For each crossing element the first fiber size is bounded by \([G:(de)(E)]\), and the second has size \(1\).

In the two-way case both constructions preserve evaluation. Equivalent inputs therefore give equivalent outputs, and each round trip returns to the original shape with the original map. This proves the asserted inverses on classes. ◻

6.2.2 Cases 3 and 4. Both mixed crossings are multiplications

Theorem 6.5 (Replacement for two multiplications). At arbitrary index, either shape evaluates to \[[g,h]\longmapsto a(g)M(bc)(h),\] where \(M\in\mathbb C[A]\) is a finite complex linear combination of group elements \(p\) satisfying \((ade)(v)p=p(bcf)(v)\) for \(v\in E\). Every such \(M\) is realized by a single diagram of either shape. The two replacements induce inverse bijections on classes modulo equality of evaluated maps.

Proof. The two backward calculations in Lemma 6.3 now run from bottom to top. Expansion gives the formula with \(M\), which is the value at \([1,1]\).

In Case 3 take \(B'=A,\ u=\mathrm{id},\ v=ad,\ w=bc\). Put the label \(M\) at the bottom mixed crossing and \(1\) at the other two. For each term \(p\) of \(M\) the first multiplication gives \([g,p(bc)(h)]\). The next multiplication and the last merge and split give \(a(g)p(bc)(h)\). In Case 4 take \(B=E,\ r=de,\ s=cf,\ t=\mathrm{id}\). Put \(M\) at the top mixed crossing and \(1\) at the other two. The first merge and split and the next multiplication give \([g,c(h)]\). Lemma 6.6 (Evaluation with one projection). On the left use \(u:G\to B\), \(v:D\to B\), and \(w:E\to B\), and labels \(r,t,s\) from bottom to top, with \(r,s\) the mixed crossing elements. 6.3.1 Case 5. Replacement by sums of right diagrams

Theorem 6.7 (Expansion into right diagrams). With this boundary, every bimodule map is a sum of right diagrams under the finite-fiber convention, with only finitely many nonzero terms on each input. A finite sum suffices exactly when only finitely many \(G\times F\)-orbits of input–output basis pairs have nonzero matrix coefficient. The group acts simultaneously on the input and output.

Proof. Let \(G\times F\) act on each basis by left multiplication by \(G\) and right multiplication by the inverse of \(F\), using the boundary embeddings. The action on input–output pairs acts on both coordinates. A bimodule map has constant matrix coefficient on each orbit.

Choose a representative \(([1,r]_A,[t,s]_C)\) of an orbit. Let \(P\le F\) be the image of its stabilizer in \(F\). In detail, \(h\in P\) exactly when there exist \(i(h)\in A,\ k(h)\in C\) satisfying \[\begin{gathered} r d(h)r^{-1}=b(i(h)),\qquad s f(h)s^{-1}=e(k(h)),\\ a(i(h))=t c(k(h))t^{-1}. \end{gathered}\] These equations describe the stabilizer as the graph \(\{(a(i(h)),h):h\in P\}\). They also make \(i,k\) conjugation embeddings. Take \(B'=P,\ j\) the inclusion, and labels \((r,t,s)\). The displayed equations are the three vertex conditions.

The pairs in the orbit have the form \[\bigl([g',r d(h)]_A,\ [g't,s f(h)]_C\bigr) \qquad(g'\in G,\ h\in F).\] The source in this pair equals \([g,x]_A\) exactly when \(g'=ga(\alpha)\) and \(x=b(\alpha)r d(h)\) for some \(\alpha\in A\). Two such choices give the same pair exactly when they are equivalent in \(A\times_P F\), by the full stabilizer description. Thus (6.4) sums each output in the orbit once. If this orbit has nonzero coefficient in a bimodule map, it has finitely many outputs over each input, so the lower projection is defined.

Conversely, (6.4) shows that a group-element triple contributes to at most one orbit, irrespective of the chosen internal group. Finite sums and finite linear labels therefore involve finitely many orbits. The construction gives one diagram for each orbit of nonzero coefficient. At each input only finitely many of these diagrams contribute, because an element of the target has finite support. This gives the asserted expansion. ◻

7 Finite parabolic subgroups

We compute conjugates, intersections, and subgroups generated by unions of finite parabolics. Block counts give explicit double-coset representatives and Mackey summands. The closure properties and the Mackey formula give a bicategory of finite parabolic groups.

Let \(S_\infty\) be the group of finitely supported permutations of \(\mathbb N\). A finite parabolic subgroup is a product \(\prod_{i\in I}\operatorname{Sym}(X_i)\), where the \(X_i\) are pairwise disjoint finite subsets of \(\mathbb N\) and \(I\) is finite. Each factor fixes the complement of \(X_i\) pointwise. Omit empty blocks. The trivial group is allowed. When comparing two such groups, take a finite set \(X\) containing both unions of blocks and add singleton blocks so that both collections partition \(X\).

Proposition 7.1 (Closure of finite parabolics). Finite parabolic subgroups are closed under conjugation in \(S_\infty\), pairwise intersections, and forming the subgroup generated by two of them.

Proof. Conjugation. If \(H=\prod_{i\in I}\operatorname{Sym}(X_i)\) and \(\alpha\in S_\infty\), then \[{}^{\alpha}\!H=\prod_{i\in I}\operatorname{Sym}(\alpha(X_i)).\] Indeed, \((\alpha h\alpha^{-1})(\alpha(x))=\alpha(h(x))\) for \(x\in X_i\). This preserves \(\alpha(X_i)\) for every \(h\in H\). Conversely, a permutation preserving these images and fixing their complement conjugates back to a permutation in \(H\).

Intersection. For \(A=\prod_{i\in I}\operatorname{Sym}(A_i)\) and \(B=\prod_{j\in J}\operatorname{Sym}(B_j)\), use blocks that partition the same finite set \(X\). A permutation of \(X\) preserves every \(A_i\) and every \(B_j\) exactly when it preserves every \(A_i\cap B_j\). Thus \[A\cap B=\prod_{i\in I}\prod_{j\in J}\operatorname{Sym}(A_i\cap B_j),\] where empty intersections contribute trivial factors.

Subgroup generated by a union. Join \(A_i\) to \(B_j\) in a graph whenever \(A_i\cap B_j\ne\varnothing\). For each connected component, let \(\widetilde{AB}^{(k)}\) be the union of its blocks. Each point lies in an \(A\)-block and a \(B\)-block joined by an edge, so either side gives the same union. The components give the minimal nonempty collections of blocks on the two sides with a common union, since any such collection contains every neighbor of each selected block.

Both \(A\) and \(B\) preserve each \(\widetilde{AB}^{(k)}\). Inside \(\widetilde{AB}^{(k)}\), a path of overlapping blocks joins any two points. Choosing a point in each overlap gives a chain whose consecutive transpositions belong to \(A\) or \(B\). After removing repetitions, successive applications of \((x\,z)=(y\,z)(x\,y)(y\,z)\) give the transposition of the endpoints. Since transpositions generate each symmetric group, the join is \(\langle A\cup B\rangle=\prod_k\operatorname{Sym}(\widetilde{AB}^{(k)})\). ◻

Lemma 7.2 (Parabolic subgroups below and above a finite group). Let \(P\le S_\infty\) be finite. Its largest parabolic subgroup is generated by all transpositions in \(P\). Its smallest parabolic overgroup is the product of the symmetric groups on its nonsingleton orbits.

Proof. Each transposition in \(P\) generates a parabolic subgroup. Their join is parabolic by Proposition 7.1 and lies in \(P\). Every parabolic subgroup of \(P\) is generated by transpositions in \(P\), giving maximality. For the overgroup, \(P\) acts on a finite set containing the supports of its elements and preserves every orbit on it. The product on the nonsingleton orbits contains \(P\). Each block of any parabolic overgroup of \(P\) is preserved by \(P\), so each orbit lies in one block. The stated product therefore lies in every parabolic overgroup. ◻

7.1 Examples

Example 7.1 (Conjugation, intersection, and generation). Take \[\begin{aligned} A&=\operatorname{Sym}(\{1,2,3\})\times\operatorname{Sym}(\{4,5\}),\\ B&=\operatorname{Sym}(\{2,3,4\})\times\operatorname{Sym}(\{6,7\}). \end{aligned}\] Use singleton blocks for the remaining points in \(\{1,\ldots,7\}\).

Conjugation. For \(\alpha=(1\,4\,6)\), the two nonsingleton blocks of \(A\) map to \(\{2,3,4\}\) and \(\{5,6\}\), so \[{}^{\alpha}\!A= \operatorname{Sym}(\{2,3,4\})\times\operatorname{Sym}(\{5,6\}).\]

Intersection. The only intersection of blocks with at least two points is \(\{1,2,3\}\cap\{2,3,4\}=\{2,3\}\). Hence \(A\cap B=\operatorname{Sym}(\{2,3\})\).

Subgroup generated by a union. The block \(\{2,3,4\}\) overlaps both nonsingleton blocks of \(A\), so their common union is \(\{1,2,3,4,5\}\). The block \(\{6,7\}\) meets only its two singleton blocks on the \(A\) side. Thus \[\langle A\cup B\rangle =\operatorname{Sym}(\{1,2,3,4,5\})\times\operatorname{Sym}(\{6,7\}).\] For instance, the join contains \((1\,5)\), obtained by taking endpoint transpositions along a path of overlapping blocks.

7.2 Double cosets from tableaux

First let \(G=\operatorname{Sym}(X)\). Write \(K=\prod_i\operatorname{Sym}(X_i)\) and \(H=\prod_j\operatorname{Sym}(Y_j)\), with singleton blocks added so that both partitions cover \(X\). Order each list of blocks and order the points within each block by their natural order in \(\mathbb N\).

Make a tableau whose \(i\)-th row has positions labeled by the points of \(X_i\). In the initial tableau \(t_0\), the box at position \(x\) has entry \(j\) if \(x\in Y_j\). A permutation \(\sigma\in G\) sends the entry at \(x\) to position \(\sigma(x)\). Write \(R_{ij}\) for the number of entries \(j\) in row \(i\) of \(\sigma t_0\).

The classification is due to James and Kerber [4, Thm. 1.3.10].

Proposition 7.3 (Double coset representatives). The map \[K\sigma H\longmapsto (R_{ij}), \qquad R_{ij}=|X_i\cap\sigma(Y_j)|\] is a bijection onto the nonnegative integer matrices with row sums \(|X_i|\) and column sums \(|Y_j|\). For a given matrix, construct a representative as follows.

  1. Split the ordered block \(X_i\) into consecutive pieces of sizes \(R_{i1},R_{i2},\ldots\). The \(j\)-th piece consists of the positions that will carry entry \(j\).

  2. Split the ordered block \(Y_j\) into consecutive pieces of sizes \(R_{1j},R_{2j},\ldots\). Map its \(i\)-th piece in order to the \(j\)-th piece of \(X_i\).

The matched pieces partition \(X\) on each side, so the prescribed maps give a permutation. It sends exactly \(R_{ij}\) points from \(Y_j\) into \(X_i\).

For any representative \(\sigma\), the Mackey intersection is \[K\cap\sigma H\sigma^{-1} =\prod_{i,j}\operatorname{Sym}(X_i\cap\sigma(Y_j)).\] This is the block-intersection formula of Proposition 7.1 applied to \(K\) and \(\sigma H\sigma^{-1}\).

Example 7.2 (A double-coset representative from a tableau). In \(S_6\) use the two subgroups \[\begin{aligned} K&=\operatorname{Sym}(\{1,2,4,5\})\times\operatorname{Sym}(\{3,6\}),\\ H&=\operatorname{Sym}(\{1,3,5\})\times\operatorname{Sym}(\{2,4,6\}). \end{aligned}\] For the matrix \(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&3\\2&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\), the first row receives one \(1\) at position \(1\). The second row receives two \(1\)’s at positions \(3,6\). The ordered matching gives \(\sigma=(5\,6)\), and its Mackey group is \(\operatorname{Sym}(\{2,4,5\})\times\operatorname{Sym}(\{3,6\})\).

Example 7.3 (One-sided coset representatives). Taking \(H=1\), with each \(Y_j\) a singleton, gives representatives for the right cosets \(K\sigma\). Their inverses give representatives for the left cosets \(\sigma^{-1}K\).

7.3 Two blocks and products

When \(K\) and \(H\) each have two blocks in \(\operatorname{Sym}(X)\), write \(n=|X|\), \(c=|X_1|\), and \(d=|Y_1|\). The matrices are precisely \[\begin{pmatrix} a&c-a\\ d-a&n-c-d+a \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \max(0,c+d-n)\le a\le\min(c,d),\qquad a\in\mathbb Z.\] The entry \(a\) determines the other three entries, and the bounds make all four entries nonnegative.

For standard two-block subgroups of \(S_n\), take \(K=S_{n_1}\times S_{n-n_1}\) and \(H=S_{n_2}\times S_{n-n_2}\), where \(1\le n_1,n_2<n\) and each first block begins at \(1\). An alternative set of representatives, convenient for computing the intersections, is \[\begin{gathered} m=\min(n_1,n_2,n-\max(n_1,n_2)),\qquad \sigma_0=1,\\ \sigma_i=(\min(n_1,n_2)-i+1,\ \max(n_1,n_2)+i)\,\sigma_{i-1} \qquad (1\le i\le m). \end{gathered}\] The transpositions are disjoint. Each one takes a further point of \(Y_1\) out of \(X_1\) and puts a point of its complement in \(X_1\). Thus \(a_i=\min(n_1,n_2)-i\) takes every allowed value exactly once. The four Mackey block sizes, in row order, are \[a_i,\qquad n_1-a_i,\qquad n_2-a_i,\qquad n-n_1-n_2+a_i.\] The four intersections are intervals in the standard order, with empty intervals omitted.

Example 7.4 (Equal two-block subgroups). For \(K=H=S_n\times S_m\le S_{n+m}\), \(n\ge m\), the representatives swap the last \(i\) points of the first block with the first \(i\) points of the second block in reverse order. The intersection group is \(S_{n-i}\times S_i\times S_i\times S_{m-i}\), with a zero-size factor interpreted as the trivial group.

Example 7.5 (Conjugation exchanging two factors). Even when \(\sigma_i^{-1}L_i\sigma_i=L_i\), the conjugation can exchange factors. For \(n=m=2\), the representative with \(i=2\) exchanges the two \(S_2\) factors.

Now let \(G=\prod_i G_i\) be a finite parabolic group. On the finite set partitioned by the blocks of \(G\), each block of \(K\) or \(H\) lies inside a block of \(G\), since every transposition within it belongs to \(G\). Thus \(K=\prod_i K_i\), \(H=\prod_i H_i\), where \(K_i,H_i\le G_i\) are the restrictions to that block. Double coset equality is componentwise, giving \[K\backslash G/H \cong \prod_i K_i\backslash G_i/H_i .\] Choose one matrix and its representative in each \(G_i\) and multiply those representatives, whose supports are disjoint. This gives every double coset once. A factor with \(K_i=G_i\) or \(H_i=G_i\) contributes only the representative \(1\).

Example 7.6 (A product with one double coset). For \(G=S_4\times S_4\), \(K=(S_2\times S_2)\times S_4\) and \(H=S_4\times(S_2\times S_2)\), each ambient block contributes one double coset. The representative is \(1\), and \(K\cap H=S_2^4\).

Example 7.7 (A product with three double cosets). For \(G=S_4^3\) on three successive blocks, \(K=(S_2\times S_2)\times S_4\times(S_2\times S_2)\) and \(H=S_4\times(S_2\times S_2)\times(S_2\times S_2)\), the first two blocks again contribute \(1\). In the last block choose \(\sigma_0=1\), \(\sigma_1=(10\,11)\), and \(\sigma_2=(9\,12)(10\,11)\). The Mackey groups are \(L_0=L_2=S_2^6\) and \(L_1=S_2^4\). Conjugation by \(\sigma_2\) exchanges the last two \(S_2\) factors of \(L_2\).

A Mackey group refines both block partitions, with the \(H\) partition transported by the representative. A binary split on each side can yield four blocks inside a single block of \(G\).

Example 7.8 (A two-block split giving four intersections). The middle double coset of \(S_2\times S_2\backslash S_4/(S_2\times S_2)\) has four singleton blocks. Thus the family of binary refinements of \(G\) with a prescribed split size need not contain the Mackey groups.

7.4 Mackey decomposition for finite parabolics

Theorem 7.4 (Mackey decomposition with explicit parabolic representatives). Let \(K,H,G\) be finite parabolics in \(S_\infty\), and let \({}^{\alpha}\!K,{}^{\beta}\!H\le G\). Index the double cosets \[J=({}^{\alpha}\!K)\backslash G/({}^{\beta}\!H), \qquad G=\bigsqcup_{j\in J}({}^{\alpha}\!K)\sigma_j({}^{\beta}\!H)\] by the tuples of matrices for the blocks of \(G\), with \(\sigma_j\) obtained by the ordered matching of Proposition 7.3. Put \[L_j={}^{\alpha}\!K\cap{}^{\sigma_j\beta}\!H, \qquad \lambda_j(\ell)=\alpha^{-1}\ell\alpha,\qquad \mu_j(\ell)=\beta^{-1}\sigma_j^{-1}\ell\sigma_j\beta .\] Then \(L_j\) is the product of the symmetric groups on the corresponding block intersections, and there is a natural isomorphism \[\operatorname{Res}_{\alpha_*}\circ\operatorname{Ind}_{\beta_*} \cong\bigoplus_{j\in J}\operatorname{Ind}_{\lambda_j}\circ\operatorname{Res}_{\mu_j}.\] For inclusion boundary embeddings this is \[\operatorname{Res}_K^G\circ\operatorname{Ind}_H^G \cong \bigoplus_{j\in K\backslash G/H} \operatorname{Ind}_{L_j}^K\circ c_{\sigma_j}\circ \operatorname{Res}_{\sigma_j^{-1}L_j\sigma_j}^{H},\] where \(c_{\sigma_j}\) gives \(\ell\in L_j\) the action of \(\sigma_j^{-1}\ell\sigma_j\).

Proof. Conjugation preserves the parabolic subgroups. The ordered matching in each block of \(G\) gives the double cosets, and the block-intersection formula computes \(L_j\). Apply Theorem 2.2 to these representatives, using the two displayed embeddings of \(L_j\). For inclusions, \(\mu_j\) is conjugation by \(\sigma_j^{-1}\) into its image followed by inclusion in \(H\), giving the stated factorization of restriction. ◻

7.5 The finite-parabolic bimodule bicategory

Khovanov considers a bicategory generated by induction and restriction between finite groups [5, § 3.2]. The closure and Mackey results above give a version for finite parabolics with one intermediate group per summand.

Corollary 7.5 (The finite-parabolic bimodule bicategory). Finite parabolic subgroups of \(S_\infty\) form a bicategory with 1-morphisms \(H\to K\) the bimodules isomorphic to finite direct sums of \[\mathbb C[K]\otimes_{\mathbb C[L]}\mathbb C[H], \qquad K\xleftarrow{a}L\xrightarrow{b}H,\] where \(L\) is finite parabolic and \(a,b\) are conjugation embeddings in \(S_\infty\). The 2-morphisms are bimodule maps. Composition is tensor product over the group algebra of the common group.

Proof. With either leg the identity, the displayed bimodule is an induction or restriction strand. With both legs the identity at \(H\), it is the regular bimodule \(\mathbb C[H]\).

For composition with \(H\xleftarrow{c}M\xrightarrow{d}J\), apply Theorem 7.4 to the middle factor \(\mathbb C[H]\) with its actions through \(b\) and \(c\). For each representative \(r\in H\) of a double coset in \(b(L)\backslash H/c(M)\), the resulting summand has middle group \[b(L)\cap r c(M)r^{-1}\] and embeddings \(x\mapsto a(b^{-1}(x))\) into \(K\) and \(x\mapsto d(c^{-1}(r^{-1}xr))\) into \(J\). These are conjugation embeddings, and the middle group is finite parabolic by Proposition 7.1. Theorem 7.4 also gives explicit representatives \(r\) by ordered matching on the blocks of \(H\). There are finitely many double cosets, and tensor product distributes over finite direct sums. The associativity and unit isomorphisms are those of bimodule tensor product. ◻

Proposition 7.1 also gives a least parabolic group containing any finite collection of parabolic images. It is the subgroup generated by their union, called their join. If all the images lie in a prescribed group, so does their join. Section 8 uses joins to construct common internal regions for Reidemeister moves.

8 Reidemeister identities for finite parabolics

We apply the block computations to the three types of Reidemeister moves. They give curl coefficients, common internal groups for two crossings, and explicit choices or obstructions for three-crossing replacements, including their multiplicities over general coefficient rings.

We work in the bicategory of Section 7.5, with \(U=S_\infty\) and all region groups finite parabolic. We use the diagrams and labels of Sections 4–6.

8.1 Reidemeister I

Proposition 8.1 (Curls from block refinements). In Theorem 4.1, write \(H=\prod_{i\in I}\operatorname{Sym}(X_i)\) and \({}^{\delta}\!K=\prod_{j\in J}\operatorname{Sym}(Y_j)\), using singleton blocks to partition the same finite set. The right up-curl and left down-curl have scalar \[[H:{}^{\delta}\!K] =\prod_{i\in I}\frac{|X_i|!}{\prod_{j:Y_j\subseteq X_i}|Y_j|!}.\] Merging two blocks of sizes \(a,b\) gives a scalar \(\binom{a+b}{a}\). A sequence of such merges factors the curl through the intermediate parabolics, and the product of its scalars is independent of the merge order. The other two curls have scalar \(1\).

Proof. A transposition of two points of \(Y_j\) belongs to \(H\), so both points lie in the same \(X_i\). Thus the \(Y_j\) refine the \(X_i\), including the singleton blocks. The index formula follows by dividing the orders of the block factors. A single merge replaces \(a!b!\) by \((a+b)!\).

For the factorization diagram use the image \(A={}^{\delta}\!K\) as the internal group and the inclusion into \(H\). To factor the curl through an intermediate group \({}^{\delta}\!K\le B\le H\), choose representatives \(r\) of the right cosets of \({}^{\delta}\!K\) in \(B\) and \(t\) of the right cosets of \(B\) in \(H\). The products \(rt\) represent the right cosets of \({}^{\delta}\!K\) in \(H\) exactly once. In the up-curl calculation the cup and cap then contribute \[\sum_t\sum_r x\,\alpha_*(t^{-1})\alpha_*(r^{-1}) \alpha_*(r)\alpha_*(t).\] The sum over \(r\) is the inner curl and contributes \([B:{}^{\delta}\!K]\). The outer cup and cap contribute \([H:B]\). In the down-curl the same product of factors acts on the left of \(x\). Iterating gives the asserted factorization. Every sequence of binary merges within the \(X_i\) has the same total index. The two curls enclosing \(G\) are the identities of Theorem 4.1. ◻

Proposition 8.2 (Curls for a join and an intersection). Fix either of the two index-valued curls on a strand and let \(c_A\) denote that curl with internal group \(A\le H\), using inclusions into \(H\). For finite parabolics \(A,B\le H\), \[c_{A\cap B}\circ c_{\langle A\cup B\rangle} =\frac{|AB|}{|\langle A\cup B\rangle|}\,(c_A\circ c_B).\] The scalar is \(1\) exactly when, on each block of \(\langle A\cup B\rangle\), at least one of the two original partitions has that whole block.

Proof. The block groups on both sides are allowed by Proposition 7.1. Apply Theorem 4.1 and use \(|AB|=|A||B|/|A\cap B|\), where \(AB\) is the set of products \(ab\). The scalar is \(1\) exactly when this set is the whole join. The product set splits over the blocks of the join. On a block where one partition has a single part, its symmetric group is already the whole factor. If both partitions split the block, choose two points in different \(B\)-parts and a permutation sending them to points in different \(A\)-parts. Swapping the two images changes the size of an intersection of an \(A\)-part with an image of a \(B\)-part. These sizes are constant on an \(A,B\) double coset, so there is more than one double coset on that block. Then \(AB\) cannot be the whole factor. ◻

Example 8.1 (Unequal products of curls). For \(H=\operatorname{Sym}(\{1,2,3\})\), \(A=\langle(12)\rangle\) and \(B=\langle(23)\rangle\), the intersection is trivial and the join is \(H\). Write \(J=\langle A\cup B\rangle\) in the diagram. The two products of curls have scalars \(6\) and \(9\), respectively.

Use the internal strand labels \(\overline{\alpha^{-1}}\) and \(\overline{\beta^{-1}\sigma_j^{-1}}\) on a common group \(L\). This choice simultaneously minimizes the indices in \[\sum_{j\in J_0}\frac{\iota_{L,j}\circ p_{L,j}}{[L_j:L]} =\sum_{j\in J_0}\iota_j\circ p_j .\]

The left label is fixed, and the right label for \(j\) equals the reference label exactly when \(\sigma_{j_0}\sigma_j^{-1}\in C_{S_\infty}(L)\). Example 8.4 (A smaller internal group reduces a projection fiber). In Case 1 take \(A=S_4\), \(G=\operatorname{Sym}(\{1,2,4\})\), \(C=\langle(12)\rangle\), \(D=F=\langle(23)\rangle\), \(E=1\) and \(p=1\), with inclusions. The least right region is \(\operatorname{Sym}(\{1,2,3\})\). Its intersection with \(G\) is \(C\), so the middle projection has fiber size \(1\) on its image. With the right region \(A\), that fiber size is \(3\).

The bounds are for one crossing element. For several crossing elements \(p\) with the same two dots \(\ell=p_0^{-1}\) and \(m=1\) on the right, a single diagram can put all coefficients at the third dot. The least region for this placement is the smallest parabolic subgroup of \(A\) containing \[(ad)(C),\qquad p_0(bc)(F)p_0^{-1},\qquad pp_0^{-1} \quad\text{for every crossing element \(p\)}.\] Indeed, the third dot must contain \(pp_0^{-1}\) since \(p=u(n)p_0\). Conversely, take the inclusion \(u\), \(v=ad\), \(w=\operatorname{Ad}_{p_0}\circ bc\), and put the coefficients on those elements \(n=pp_0^{-1}\). Apply Lemma 7.2 to the subgroup generated by the displayed elements and subgroups.

Proposition 8.7 (Coefficient-one expansion in Case 5). A left diagram in Case 5 with group-element dots is a sum of right diagrams, one for each supported orbit of input–output pairs, each with coefficient \(1\). Write \(\mathcal S\) for this set of orbits, \(P_{\mathcal O}\) for each projected stabilizer, and \(T_{\mathcal O}\) for the matrix with coefficient \(1\) on \(\mathcal O\) and \(0\) elsewhere. For any orbit of input–output pairs, let \(P\le F\) be the projection of its stabilizer. Among single right diagrams with group-element dots realizing this orbit, the least multiplicity is \([P:L]\), where \(L\) is generated by all transpositions in \(P\). It equals \(1\) exactly when \(P\) is parabolic.

Proof. In the notation of Lemma 6.6, the left equation compares the two maps \[[g,x]_A\longmapsto u(g)r v(x)t,\qquad [h,y]_C\longmapsto u(h)s w(y).\] Both are equivariant for the action of \(G\times F\) on \(B\) given by \((q,k)\cdot z=u(q)z\,w(f(k))^{-1}\). This follows from the three vertex equations, including \(vd=\operatorname{Ad}_t\circ wf\). A supported pair has the same image \(z\) under the two maps. If \(k\in F\) lies in both individual stabilizer projections, the corresponding \(G\)-components \(q_1,q_2\) satisfy \[u(q_1)=z\,w(f(k))\,z^{-1}=u(q_2).\] Since \(u\) is injective, \(q_1=q_2\). Hence the projected pair stabilizer is exactly the intersection of the two individual projections. For a representative \(([1,x]_A,[h,y]_C)\), those projections are \(d^{-1}(x^{-1}b(A)x)\) and \(f^{-1}(y^{-1}e(C)y)\), with inverse images taken in \(F\). They are finite parabolics by conjugation and intersection closure. The orbit term in Theorem 6.7 can therefore use the full stabilizer and counts each pair once. Every left basis coefficient is \(1\) on a supported pair by (6.3).

For an arbitrary right orbit, the vertex conditions require \(j(B')\le P\). Conversely every parabolic subgroup of \(P\) works by restricting the three embeddings. Replacing \(A\times_P F\) by \(A\times_{j(B')}F\) gives \([P:j(B')]\) classes over each original class, because the balancing action is free. Thus each pair is counted exactly that many times. The largest parabolic subgroup of \(P\) from Lemma 7.2 minimizes the index. Stabilizers of other representatives project to conjugate subgroups of \(F\), so the minimum is independent of the representative. ◻

Other coefficient rings.

For these two three-crossing shapes, the same basis formulas work over any commutative ring \(R\) with identity. For each orbit \(\mathcal O\) of input–output pairs, let \(L_{\mathcal O}\) be the largest parabolic subgroup of \(P_{\mathcal O}\). The \(R\)-span of right diagrams in the space of bimodule maps is \[\bigoplus_{\mathcal O} \bigl([P_{\mathcal O}:L_{\mathcal O}]\,R\bigr)T_{\mathcal O}.\] Indeed, each multiplicity \([P_{\mathcal O}:j(B')]\) is divisible by \([P_{\mathcal O}:L_{\mathcal O}]\), and taking \(j(B')=L_{\mathcal O}\) realizes that index. The sum runs over all orbits, including those on which no left diagram is nonzero. Over \(\mathbb Z\), the quotient by this span is therefore the direct sum of the groups \(\mathbb Z/[P_{\mathcal O}:L_{\mathcal O}]\mathbb Z\). The coefficient-one expansion of a left diagram also holds over \(R\), since the coefficient count uses only the basis sets.

For linear left dots, expand into group-element triples and apply the coefficient-one identity to each term. Any right orbit can still be realized over \(\mathbb C\) with coefficient \(1\) by dividing by its least multiplicity.

Proposition 8.8 (A Case 6 obstruction and a replacement family). If the projected pair stabilizer of a right orbit is strictly smaller than the intersection of its two individual stabilizer projections, its orbit map has no left replacement, even by a finite sum.

On the other hand, suppose \(F=1\), all boundary embeddings are inclusions, \(GE\) is a subgroup, \(D\cap GE=A\), and \(G\cap E=C\). Then every right diagram has a single left replacement through the parabolic group \(\langle G\cup D\cup E\rangle\).

Proof. Proposition 8.7 shows that a group-element left term can have a nonzero coefficient on an orbit only when its projected pair stabilizer equals the intersection of the two individual projections. Every left term therefore vanishes on the indicated orbit.

For the second assertion, \(B'=1\) on the right since \(F=1\). For right dots \((r,t,s)\), take the displayed join as \(B\) on the left, the inclusion embeddings, and dots \((1,r^{-1}ts,1)\). In the left formula an output exists exactly when \(xr^{-1}\in GE\), because \(g,t,s\in GE\). As \(xr^{-1}\in D\), this is \(xr^{-1}\in A\). In that case the class \([g(xr^{-1})t,s]_C\) is the unique output, because \(G\cap E=C\). The right formula gives the same class under the same condition. For linear right dots with coefficients \(c_r,c_t,c_s\) at the three vertices, put \(\sum_{r,t,s}c_r c_t c_s r^{-1}ts\) at the middle left dot. Every term is admissible because \(F=1\). ◻

Example 8.5 (An orbit coefficient vanishing in characteristic three). Take all six boundary groups equal to \(S_3\), the identity embeddings, and right dots \((1,(123),1)\) with \(B'=1\). The individual projections are both \(S_3\), while the pair stabilizer projects to \(\langle(123)\rangle\). On the two bases identified with \(S_3\), the map sends \(x\) to \(3x((123)+(132))\), since each conjugate of \((123)\) is counted three times. Over a ring of characteristic \(3\), every right diagram has zero coefficient on this orbit.

A left replacement can also fail when the projected pair stabilizer equals the intersection of the two individual projections.

Proposition 8.9 (A Case 6 obstruction with trivial projected stabilizer). A right diagram in the finite-parabolic category can have no left replacement even when its projected pair stabilizer and both individual stabilizer projections are trivial.

Proof. Example 8.6 gives the obstruction. All three projected stabilizers are trivial because \(F=1\). ◻

Example 8.6 (A Case 6 obstruction with trivial projected stabilizers). Take \[G=A=C=\langle(12),(34)\rangle,\qquad D=E=\operatorname{Sym}(\{1,2,3,4\}),\qquad F=1\] with inclusion embeddings. The two boundary bases identify with \(D\) by multiplication. The right diagram with \(B'=1\) and unit dots fixes the basis elements of \(A\) and sends those in \(D\setminus A\) to zero by (6.4).

Consider any left diagram with group-element dots \(r,t,s\) and embeddings \(u,v,w\) as in Lemma 6.6. Its lower and upper vertex conditions give \[u(G)\le r v(D)r^{-1},\qquad u(G)\le s w(E)s^{-1}.\] The image \(u(G)\) moves four points. Both displayed overgroups are full symmetric groups on four points, so they are the same subgroup. Use \([1,x]_A\) and \([1,y]_C\) for the input and output. The left-diagram equation becomes \[s w(y)s^{-1}=(r v(x)r^{-1})(rts^{-1}).\] If \(rts^{-1}\) belongs to the common subgroup, every input has exactly one output. Otherwise every input has zero outputs. By linearity, the sum of the output coefficients of any finite sum of left diagrams is constant on basis inputs. The right diagram has coefficient sum \(1\) on \(A\) and \(0\) off \(A\), so it has no left replacement.

Example 8.7 (A left replacement through overlapping blocks). Take \(G=A=C=F=1\), \(D=\langle(12)\rangle\) and \(E=\langle(23)\rangle\). The right diagram that sends \(r\) to \(s\) and all other basis elements to zero has a left replacement with middle group \(\operatorname{Sym}(\{1,2,3\})\) and middle dot \(r^{-1}s\).
